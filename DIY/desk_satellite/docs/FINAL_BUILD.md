## The reference

A copper-wire (1 and 0.7 mm) cube body with an ESP32-C3 and a 0.96" 80 × 160 TFT showing an NTP clock, an SHT31 temperature and humidity sensor, a touch switch, a 4.2 V Type-C charging module plus a separate solar charge controller and a LiPo, mini solar cells or panels as the wings, a battery level indicator, a 3 mm brass tube post and a 6 cm bamboo coaster. ## The decisions

### A satellite at a glance: body, two wings, a light on the corner

The reference is a copper-wire cube with a clock inside, two solar wings and a dish, on a brass tube over a coaster. Ours keeps that silhouette and nothing it does not need: a matte black bus 48 × 52 × 24 mm, two 80 × 45 mm panels on short booms, a tiny warm light glued on its top front corner, an amber pool under the body on a brass base, one stainless post, one brass base. 224 mm wing tip to wing tip, 159 mm tall, about 328 g. No display, no clock, no sensor: the builder's brief is panels, a cell they keep topped up, USB as the fallback, and a 3000 K beacon at the tip that is on or blinks.

### Two 5 V wings in series, in a rectangle

The charger holds a weak input at the cell's voltage plus 0.33 V and will not charge below that, and the board adds a Schottky, so the wings must deliver 4.3 to 4.7 V in whatever light they get. One 10-cell 5 V panel opens at 6.1 V in sun and about 4 V in a bright room: too low exactly when it matters. Two in series open at 12.2 V in sun and 7 to 8 V in a room, always above the line, and the charger pulls the pair down and takes their full current, 90 mA in sun; the board is rated to 18 V. The wings changed at the builder's word on the evening of 2026-11-03: the sheet was approved with two 56 mm squares; the builder then wanted a rectangle a little smaller than the reference's 110 × 60 mm panel, and Amazon's one such panel with leads is the [80 × 45 mm 5 V 90 mA poly](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FL26KWWT) with 150 mm leads welded on: the same current as the squares, nothing to solder on the panel, 224 mm wing tip to wing tip.

### Cyber Dawn's spare charger board, straight from the drawer

Cyber Dawn bought two [bq25185 boards](https://www.adafruit.com/product/6106) and dropped solar on 2026-10-31 because a 5 W panel could not feed a clock whose display alone drinks 50 mA (its `SOLAR_FEASIBILITY.md` is the record). Here the whole satellite sleeps at 0.2 mA and blinks at 0.4, a hundred times less, so the same board and a pair of 80 × 45 mm panels close the budget at a bright window. What the board gives, from its schematic and TI's datasheet: USB-C and a 5 to 18 V VIN pad through two Schottkys, so the panels and a cable never fight; charging that follows a weak input instead of collapsing it; no false termination while it does so; a 6 h timer that a night resets; 4 µA from the cell when idle; 500 mA with the jumper cut. Its 5 V boost converter is switched off with one wire (EN to G) and its screw terminal becomes a ground point.

### A 16340 in a holder, never soldered

The sheet was approved with the owned 500 mAh pouch. The same day the builder chose a [Nitecore 16340 cell](https://imrbatteries.com/products/nitecore-nl169-16340-950mah-battery) instead, thirteen in the drawer from two IMR Batteries orders of 2026-10-16: 950 mAh, protection under the wrap, button top, 20 g. 84 days of blinking in the dark instead of 45, 10 of glow instead of 5, and a cell that lifts out of its [holder](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CRGK889F) when the lid is off. The holder is Cyber Dawn's 43 × 18 × 14 mm tray; its leads meet an owned JST-PH pigtail 40 mm from the cell, so no iron comes near it, and the plug goes into the charger's socket. The price is the box: the bus grows from 42 × 41 × 22 to 48 × 52 × 24 mm to take the tray along its front wall with the two boards behind it, both ports through the lid, and the satellite gains about 25 g.

### The controller runs from the cell through one diode, and the boost stays off

The board's BAT pad is the cell. One [SS14](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B079KJ46ZS) from it to the controller's 5V pin gives the controller 3.5 to 4.0 V, which its own regulator turns into 3.3 V, and costs 0.15 V. The diode is not for the charger: on the SuperMini the 5V pin is USB VBUS with no diode, so without it plugging a flashing cable would push 5 V straight into a lithium cell. With it, flash whenever you like. The charger's 5 V boost would be the tidy way to power a 5 V board, but it idles at about a milliamp with its green LED, five times the sleeping controller; one wire from EN to G turns it off for 37 µA through its pull-up.

### The ESP32-C3 SuperMini again, with its power LED gone

Thirteen are owned, the toolchain exists, and the firmware is a hundred lines: light sleep with a timer, a pin, a button. The chip sleeps at 0.13 mA in light sleep and wakes in a millisecond, which is what a blink wants; deep sleep would spend more rebooting every two seconds than it saves. The board's red power LED burns about 1 mA all day, five times the rest of the sleeping satellite, so it comes off with tweezers before anything is wired. An ATtiny would sleep at 1 µA and make a desk under room light nearly self-sustaining, at the price of a new toolchain and programmer; written down here as the upgrade if the window budget ever feels thin.

### The beacon: a chip glued on the corner, no legs, no mast

The first draft put a 3 mm LED on two bare rods 52 mm above the bus. The builder wanted a small light stuck on the corner, not a tall pin, with no legs at all, so the mast went the same morning and the LED became a [pre-wired 0805 chip](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09QGJ9LY7): 2 × 1.25 × 1.1 mm, with two 0.1 mm enamelled leads already soldered on. It is glued flat on the top of the bus 5 mm in from the front and the right wall, its leads run 1 mm over the surface and down a 1.2 mm pinhole, and the resistor sits inside above the controller: nothing stands up but 1.1 mm of chip, and nothing is soldered to the box. A warm-white chip drops about 2.9 V, so 100 Ω from the 3.3 V pin gives 4 to 5 mA, a clear point of light in a room (the kit's 68 Ω gives 7 mA if it looks faint; the chip takes 20); PWM dims the steady mode to about a quarter. The overall height falls from 209 to 152 mm.

### An amber pool under the body

The builder asked for a light below the satellite that shines down, in the warm yellow that matches the family's brass. The hologram's [LED filament](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FR35BGYL), the 38 mm 2200 K strip with nine spare in its pack, is Kapton-taped under the floor of the bus 10 mm ahead of the post, parallel to the wings, glowing down the post onto the brass base 118 mm below: a pool of amber on brass. GPIO1 drives it through 68 Ω, about 12 mA flat out (the hologram's figure), PWM-dimmed to 3 for the glow mode; its two leads come up through a 1.5 mm hole in the floor. It is the one thing on this satellite that is not for the satellite's own sake, and it costs: the glow mode is 3.4 mA, 10 days on the cell in the dark, so it is the window and evening mode, and the beacon alone is the one that lives on the wings.

### One post on a weighted brass base, a printed hub and box: no solder in the frame

A [brass coaster](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D9DMK3L7), 75 × 8 mm, is the base. It weighs only 80 g, a thin top over a hollow, so about 150 g of steel is epoxied into the hollow and the pad goes back over it: 230 g, all of it in the bottom 8 mm, unseen. A 12 mm printed hub glued to its flat top is the post's socket, since the top is too thin to drill. It replaced the 3 in stainless disc on 2026-11-06 at the builder's word: the steel read too white on the bench, and brass is the family's metal and the amber pool's colour. The post is one 1.5 mm coupon, 110 mm free, into a boss under the box. Each boom is a 1.5 mm rod in a 6 mm boss on the box's side wall, glued along the back of its panel. Three sockets, three drops of CA, zero frame joints, no solder on steel anywhere. Centre of mass 46 mm up on a 75 mm base: it tips at about 39°, against 30° on the steel disc and about 25° on the coaster unweighted. The box is the one printed body in the family after Cyber Dawn's, and for the same reason: a satellite bus is a box.

### What the panels really do

The charger itself takes about 0.75 mA from its input while charging, so below about 1 mA of panel current nothing is stored. Room light on a desk (500 lux) gives the pair about 0.3 mA: nothing. Daylight beside a window (1 000 to 3 000 lux) gives 0.9 to 2.7 mA, so 0.2 to 2.0 mA net, which over the charger's 6 h window is 1 to 12 mAh a day against the beacon mode's 10: a bright window carries it, a dull one does not. An overcast sill (9 mA, 50 mAh a day) banks five beacon days in one; the glow mode's 82 wants hours of direct sun or the cable. On a desk away from the window it is a 84-day battery object that gets a USB top-up; at a bright window it is a solar one. These are the listing's full-sun figure scaled by lux (its 38 000 lux was a halogen lamp, which silicon counts at nearly three daylight lux each), not measurements; the first week on the sill, with the cell voltage read each evening at the BAT pad, is the real number.

### A reference, not a copy

Huy Vector's satellite is a copper-wire cube with a clock face, a dish, a charging module beside a separate solar controller, on a brass tube over a bamboo coaster. This one keeps only the idea: a body on one post over a round base, two solar wings. Everything else is its own: a closed matte black bus instead of an open wire cube, no screen and no clock, a stainless post on a weighted brass plinth instead of a brass tube over bamboo, two panels in series into one board that is also the USB charger, a legless chip beacon on the corner where the reference has a dish, and an amber pool under the body that the reference does not have. ### How it sits with the family

Black, steel and brass: one 1.5 mm stainless post like the drone's rods, a printed black body like Cyber Dawn's, a brass base like the Pocket AIs' metal, and nothing on it that is decoration. The wings are the two biggest parts and they are the power supply; the lights are a chip on the corner and the hologram's amber filament underneath, glowing on the brass. At 224 mm wide and 159 tall it stands beside the 82 mm Cyber Dawn and the 72 mm hologram as the wide one. Prototype, not product: the lid pulls off, the cell unplugs, the firmware reflashes.

Not used: `VU`, `D+`, `D−`, `5V`, the `+` terminal. The charge-rate jumper on the back is cut (500 mA).

## The budget, honestly

From the cell: 0.22 mA asleep (controller in light sleep 0.13, regulator about 0.05, EN pull-up 0.037, charger 0.004); beacon mode 0.42 mA (4 mA for 100 ms every 2 s), 10 mAh a day, 84 days on the 950 mAh cell (850 usable) in the dark; glow mode (the pool at 3 mA) 3.4 mA, 82 mAh a day, 10 days; lamp mode (pool flat out, beacon steady) 13.4 mA, 2.6 days. The SuperMini's red power LED, if left on, adds about 1 mA and cuts the beacon mode to 25 days.

From the wings: the charger spends 0.75 mA of input on itself while charging and stops after 6 h until dark; stored means six hours of what the pair gives beyond that. The listing's 90 mA taken as a full-sun figure, scaled by lux, room LED light at 0.65 of daylight per lux; estimates until the first week on the sill is read off the BAT pad.

## What the firmware needs

- ESP-IDF 6.0.2 for the esp32c3, a new small project (no Wi-Fi, no server): `esp_pm` light sleep with the RTC timer; GPIO0 as the beacon, GPIO10 as the button with the internal pull-up, GPIO8 driven high so the blue LED stays dark.
- Three modes in NVS, cycled by a short press: off; beacon (the chip blinks 100 ms every 2 s: set the pin, sleep 100 ms, clear it, sleep 1.9 s); glow (the beacon blinks and the pool sits at about 3 mA on LEDC PWM, RC_FAST clock so it keeps running in light sleep). A long press in glow turns the pool up full and the beacon steady until the next press.
- Nothing else runs. Wi-Fi never starts; the radio's 300 mA peaks have no place in this budget. Console on USB Serial/JTAG when a cable is in.
- First-power measurements the record wants: the board's current with the power LED gone, in each mode (expected about 0.2, 0.4, 3.4 and 13 mA), the beacon's current on GPIO0 (3 to 5 mA) and the filament's on GPIO1 (about 12 mA).
- If the cell voltage is ever wanted on the beacon (a slow double-blink below 3.4 V): two resistors from BAT to GND into GPIO1, read once an hour. Not drawn; it costs two parts and 4 µA.

## Before power, with the meter

- The holder's plug against the charger's socket, before it goes in: the lead from the button-top end on the pin the board marks +, the pigtail joint in heat-shrink; meter the plug with the cell in, 3.6 to 4.1 V, right way round.
- The diode's direction, before the controller is powered: band toward the controller's 5V pin; the BAT pad shows the cell's voltage, the 5V pin shows 0.15 V less. Then the panel pair at the VIN and G pads in daylight: positive, 10 to 12 V open circuit, red lead +. Then the beacon's two leads on the diode test: it lights faintly one way; mark that lead +. The filament's + mark goes toward its resistor.
- The frame floats: post to G open, each boom to G open (the wing wires run beside the booms, so a nick would show here). Everything else is a supplier spec and is not metered.
