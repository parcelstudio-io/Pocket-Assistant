Desk satellite · step 2: the science and engineering

Why a window keeps it blinking

The working behind every number on the sheet of 2026-11-02, approved 2026-11-03 and revised the same day for the Nitecore 16340 cell: a silicon cell in room light, two wings in series, a charger that follows a weak panel and what it eats, the six hours, a day in milliamp-hours, a cell never rushed, one diode and the rail, light sleep against deep, two load lines, the glowing-body line, a hundred milliseconds, and one post over a brass base.
Read after Desk Satellite Build. Nothing measured yet; every figure is derived from datasheet and listing numbers and the sheet's dimensions, and the solar curves are a model until the first week on the sill.

The whole thing in one sentence

Two wings make a few milliamps in daylight; a charger that follows a weak panel instead of collapsing it feeds a 950 mAh cell; a controller asleep between blinks spends 0.42 mA on a warm chip at the corner and, when asked, 3 more on an amber pool below.

Everything on the approved sheet follows from that: why two wings in series, why this charger and not a cleverer one, why the cell is never rushed, why light sleep and not deep, why a resistor on each light, why one 1.5 mm post can carry it all. This page takes each in the order the sheet made them and shows the working.

What a silicon cell does in room light

A solar cell is a diode in the light. Light knocks electrons loose at a rate proportional to the light, so the short-circuit current is linear in lux: the listing's 90 mA in full sun (100 000 lux, the listing's full-sun figure) is 2.7 mA at a bright window (3 000 lux) and 0.29 mA on a desk under 500 lux of LED light. Silicon counts LED light at only 0.65 of daylight per lux, because an LED spectrum has none of the deep red and infrared that silicon converts best.

The voltage is the diode's, and it climbs with the logarithm of the light: about 77 mV per tenfold per cell for cells like these. Ten cells that open at 6.1 V in sun open at 3.8 V on the desk. The leak every small cut cell has (the model gives each panel 20 kΩ) pulls the dim-light voltage down further and takes about 0.1 mA off the top at the charger's voltage, whatever the light.

The charger does not care about the voltage above its line. It pulls the wings down to 4.3 to 4.7 V (the cell, plus 0.33 V of tracking, plus 0.2 V in the board's Schottky) and takes the current that flows there. On every curve that point is on the flat top, so the wings behave as a current source and the current is simply the light.

Why two wings in series, and what that costs

The one thing the charger needs is 4.3 to 4.7 V at its pad, depending on how full the cell is. One wing alone opens at 3.8 V on the desk and 4.9 V at a bright window: nothing on the desk, and at the window a cliff a few tenths of a volt past the operating point, where a little shade would push it over. Two in series open at 7.7 V in the same room light and 9.8 at the window, and the operating point sits on the flat top in any light the satellite will see.

The cost is current. In series the pair gives one wing's current; in parallel two wings would give two, but only when each can reach the line on its own, and with a full cell the line is at 4.7 V. Keeping a full cell full at a dull window (1 000 lux) the series pair gives 0.8 mA and a parallel pair nothing at all; at a bright window (3 000 lux) still 2.6 against 1.9; only on a sunny sill does parallel pull ahead, 14.5 mA against 8.9, and in sun nearly double. The sheet chose the arrangement that never reads zero at a window over the one that gives more on a sill, because keeping a full cell full in dull light is the job, and the bright end has power to spare.

There is a third way, and it is the upgrade if the window ever feels thin. A switching charger that tracks the pair's maximum-power point trades their spare volts for amps: from the same wings at 3 000 lux it would push about 4 mA into the cell instead of 2.6, 1.7 times as much, and 206 mA in sun. The owned board is linear and cannot. It was chosen because it is owned, because it does USB from the same socket, and because the budget closes without the extra.

How the charger follows a weak wing, and why it eats 0.75 mA

A linear charger is a controlled resistor between its input and the cell. This one watches its input and, when the source is weak, throttles its own current so the input never falls below the cell plus 0.33 V. That is battery tracking, and it is exactly right for a panel: it keeps the wings just above the cell, on the flat top of their curve, where their current is greatest. A charger without it would ask a weak panel for more than it has, the panel's voltage would collapse below the cell, charging would stop, the voltage would recover, and it would chatter.

The same loop keeps the charger cold on solar. The drop across it is 0.33 V and the board's Schottky another 0.2: 48 mW at the full 90 mA. A plain linear charger holding a 12 V pair at the cell's 3.8 V would burn 0.76 W. From USB the drop is 5 V minus the cell at 500 mA, 0.6 W, which inside a closed printed box of 98 cm² warms the box about 6 °C; the chip throttles itself at a 125 °C junction and never gets there.

Two of the sheet's rules come out of this loop. While tracking, the charger does not terminate: the current is small because the light is weak, not because the cell is full, and the chip cannot tell the two apart, so it holds the cell at 4.2 V as long as there is light. And the charger spends about 0.75 mA from its input on its own regulator and references whenever an input is present, so the wings must beat that before anything reaches the cell. On the desk they do not.

Where the six hours come from

Every lithium charger has a safety timer: if the cell has not reached full in a set time, something is wrong (a dead cell, a short, a cell that is not there) and it stops. This one fixes it at 6 h, because from a wall adapter a 500 mA charge of this cell takes about 2.2 h and six is 2.7 times that. The timer was designed for a cable, not a window.

On the wings it starts when the morning first lifts the input above the cell plus 0.33 V and runs out six hours later, whatever the sun is doing; it resets only when the input disappears, which on a window means dusk. So a day on the sill is six hours of harvest, not twelve, and the sheet's table counts six. Cutting the input would restart it, but the board gives the firmware no pin for that without another part, so it is not wired. At a bright window six hours are just enough: 3 000 lux stores 11 mAh against the beacon's 10 a day.

A day on the sill, in milliamp-hours

Add it up at the sheet's five light levels. Stored is the pair's current at 4.3 V, less the charger's 0.75 mA, times six hours; spent is the mode's current times twenty-four. The beacon costs 10 mAh a day, the glow 82, the lamp 322.

The honest reading: on a desk away from the window this is a 84-day battery object with a USB top-up; beside a bright window it is a solar one in beacon mode; on a sill it banks beacon days; the glow and the lamp live on direct sun or the cable. All of it is listing figures through a model until the first week on the sill is read off the BAT pad each evening, and the model's leak makes these numbers a shade lower than the sheet's table, which left it out.

The cell: a 16340 that must never be rushed

The cell is a Nitecore 16340: a lithium-ion cylinder 16.6 mm across and 34.1 long, 3.6 V nominal, 4.2 full, 950 mAh, with its protection circuit under the wrap and a button top. The builder bought seven on 2026-11-03 and chose it over the 500 mAh pouch the sheet was approved with: nearly double the days in the dark, and a cell that lifts out of its holder when the lid is off. The protection opens the circuit above about 4.25 V, below about 2.8 V and above a couple of amps, and reconnects when the fault clears; in this build the controller's 3.25 V floor comes first, so the cell's own cut-off is a last line it never reaches.

Lithium cells are charged at a rate of their own capacity. 1 C is 950 mA for this cell; the board's jumper is cut to 500 mA, 0.53 C, what a 16340 charger feeds it. Faster plates lithium metal onto the graphite instead of slipping it between the layers; so does charging below 0 °C, which a desk does not do, and the board's thermistor pin is fixed so there is no sensor to say otherwise. From the wings the charge is 90 mA at the very most, a tenth of 1 C.

The holder is a plastic tray with two spring contacts, and the springs are the only resistance in the path: a few tens of milliohms, a few millivolts at the charge current and nothing at all at the beacon's. A cell held at 4.2 V in the light every day ages faster than one that is cycled: the full-charge voltage slowly oxidises the electrolyte at the positive electrode. Expect the capacity to fade over a year or two on a sunny sill, and a $10 cell to swap in; calendar ageing at full charge is the one cost of a charger that never terminates on solar, paid gladly for a cell that is always full when dark comes.

One diode, and the rail as the cell empties

The SuperMini has no battery input. Its 5V pin is the USB connector's VBUS pin with nothing in between, so a cable plugged into the controller would put 5 V on anything wired to that pin, here the charger's BAT pad and the cell. One Schottky between the pad and the pin, band toward the controller, lets the cell feed the board and lets nothing feed back; it drops 0.15 V at this milliamp.

Behind the pin a small regulator makes the 3.3 V rail. It needs about 0.1 V of headroom, so the rail holds 3.3 V while the cell is above 3.55 V and follows the cell down, 0.25 V behind, below that. The chip is specified to 3.0 V, which is a 3.25 V cell, and keeps running out of spec until its own brown-out reset near 2.7 V. Below 3.25 V the cell has a few per cent left, so the floor costs nothing the protection cut was not already taking; the sheet's 850 of 950 keeps a margin beyond.

The lights feel the sag before the chip does. A resistor from a 3.05 V rail gives the chip 2.7 mA instead of 4.4, a dimmer blink that is itself the low-cell warning; the budget's 4 mA is the full-rail figure, so the days in the dark are, if anything, a little more.

Light sleep against deep sleep for a two-second blink

The ESP32-C3 has three sleeps and no coprocessor: unlike the ESP32-S3 it has no ULP to blink a pin while the main chip is off, so the blink is the chip's own job. Modem sleep keeps the CPU running at 20 mA. Deep sleep is 5 µA with everything off but the clock, and waking is a reboot: about 250 ms through the ROM and the flash at some 30 mA, which every two seconds averages 3.75 mA.

Light sleep is 0.13 mA with the clocks gated and the RAM and the pins held, and waking takes under a millisecond. The blink is then four lines: set the pin, sleep 100 ms, clear it, sleep 1.9 s; 0.42 mA all in with the chip's 4 mA. Deep sleep would win only if the period were longer than about 60 s.

The pool is PWM from the LED-control peripheral clocked by the chip's internal RC oscillator, which keeps running in light sleep, so the glow mode is the same sleep with one timer alive. Wi-Fi never starts: its 300 mA peaks would need the boost the sheet turns off, and nothing here has anything to say to a network.

A phosphor chip at 4 mA, a filament at 10

An LED is a diode: its current climbs exponentially with the voltage across it, about tenfold for every 196 mV, so a pin that set a voltage would set a current it could not control. A resistor in series fixes it. The pin gives 3.3 V, the resistor and the pin's own 25 Ω take the difference, and the diode's curve and the resistor's line cross at one point. For the chip, 2.75 V at a few milliamps and 100 Ω, that is 4.4 mA, a fifth of its 20 and about 38 millicandela: at 3 m across a dark room, 30 times Venus at its brightest. For the filament, the hologram's figure again: 2.4 V, 68 Ω, 9.7 mA, about 2.5 lumens, a candle's fifth spread over the brass.

The chip is a 0.3 mm blue die on a 2 × 1.25 mm pad under a drop of silicone loaded with yellow and red phosphor, with two 0.1 mm enamelled wires soldered on at the factory; the enamel burns off when the ends are tinned, and the glue that holds it to the box carries no current. The model's chip reads 2.88 V at 20 mA; the listing says 2.8 to 3.3, the one number it leaves loose, which is why the sheet asks for the chip to be lit through its resistor on the bench before it is glued: a high-voltage chip would be dim, and the kit's 68 Ω is the fix.

The filament is the same physics on a 38 mm ceramic strip: a row of blue dies under a thick orange phosphor, both leads at one end, a polarity mark. The hologram's physics page has its full curve; here it only matters that 68 Ω holds it under the pin's 20 mA with a low-voltage strip and that it is never connected without the resistor.

Why 3000 K reads warm, and why a 2200 K pool suits brass

Colour temperature is the colour of a glowing body: a candle flame at 1 850 K, a tungsten bulb at 2 700, a halogen desk lamp at 3 000, the midday sun at 5 500, an overcast sky at 6 500. Lower is redder, because a cooler body radiates less blue. An LED has no temperature. It is a blue die whose light is partly turned yellow and red by phosphor, and the maker mixes the phosphor until the colour lands on the glowing-body line at the kelvin on the box; more red phosphor, lower kelvin, warmer.

3000 K reads warm because it is a halogen lamp's colour, and 2200 K is a candle's, which is why the hologram chose it for the pool on the desk. Brass reflects red and yellow far better than blue, so the pool on the base comes back a shade more golden than the filament itself. Polished brass under room light reads near 3 000 K on the line; shaded or old brass, the kind on the family's earlier builds, nearer 2 200 to 2 500. The pool is that brass at its warm end, in light instead of metal, which is what the builder asked for.

A hundred milliseconds every two seconds

The eye adds up light for about a tenth of a second. Within that window a flash twice as long looks twice as bright (Bloch's law); past it, a longer flash looks no brighter, only longer. So 100 ms is the shortest blink that looks as bright as a steady light, and 5 % duty buys the full brightness at a twentieth of the power: the beacon at 0.2 mA average looks like a 4 mA light that is mostly off.

Two seconds is the cadence of a thing passing, slow enough to be calm and quick enough that a glance catches one. Aircraft strobes flash for a few tens of milliseconds about once a second, which is why they look sharp but thin; a satellite tumbling across a dark sky flashes every few seconds as a panel catches the sun. The steady modes dim by PWM at 5 kHz, far above the 60 Hz the eye can follow, so dimming shows as brightness and not flicker; and because the eye compresses brightness roughly as a cube root, 30 % duty looks about two thirds as bright, not a third.

The post as a column, the base as the fuse

The post is one 1.5 mm 304 rod with 110 mm free between its two sockets, carrying 97 g. As a column fixed at the bottom and free at the top it buckles at 9.8 N; it carries 0.95, a 10-fold margin. Its softness is sideways. A rod's stiffness goes as the fourth power of its diameter and the cube of its free length, so a 1 N push at the top bends this one 9 mm, and with 97 g on it the satellite sways at about 5.3 Hz when nudged, settling in a second or two.

That is the live feel of a thing on a post, and it is elastic. The steel would take a set only past about 1.5 N at the top, and long before that, at 0.76 N, the whole satellite tips over the edge of its base. The base is the fuse: the post cannot be bent on the desk, only knocked over, and it lands on a wing of epoxy and a box of PETG.

The 230 g base, 80 g of brass and 150 of steel hidden under it, is 70 % of the mass and all of the stability: centre of mass 46 mm up on a 37.5 mm radius, so it tips at 39°. The booms carry 16 g each on 80 mm and sag 0.08 mm, the panel glued along the rod stiffening both. Nothing is soldered because nothing needs to be: three rods in three glued sockets, a 1.5 mm rod in a 1.6 mm hole, the glue cured a day.
