Three prototypes for the brass-and-ivory desk robot

Crumb’s parts have been on the bench since 2026-11-03: the two gear motors, the I²C motor driver, three colour screens, the amplifiers; its controller lands by 11-11. These three keep every one of them and add what you asked for today: a frame that is not Mo-chan’s wire hexagon, ivory wheels, a camera, a real spinning lidar, a speaker and a microphone, in white, ivory, brass and black only. The electronics are the same in all three. What changes is the body, the face, and where the lidar sits. Pick one and the pin-to-pin wiring sheet follows.

You asked for

  • Brass-coloured, with ivory wheels; cute; compact.
  • A frame that is its own, not Mo-chan’s copper hexagon.
  • A camera, a lidar, a small speaker and a microphone.
  • Three prototype drawings before anything else.

Already in Crumb’s bin

  • XIAO ESP32-S3 Sense (camera, microphone; by 11-11), 1.54" colour screens ×3, N20 gear motors ×2, I²C motor driver, I²S amplifier.
  • From shared stock: 16340 cell, holder, toggle, speaker, fuel gauge, single-point laser ranger, motion sensor (3-pack ordered 11-07), 0.96" OLEDs (7 spare), 1.5 mm and 1.0 mm brass rod, canvas-white spray (Pip’s ivory), cardstock, clear tape.
  • Set aside, back to the shared bin: the two 64-tooth steel gears (ivory wheels replace them) and the 8×8 ranger (the lidar does its job).

New, the same in all three

  • The lidar: LDROBOT STL-19P as the Waveshare D500 kit (Amazon; $95.99 at Waveshare, $99.99 at Hiwonder).
  • One pair of Pololu white wheels: 32 mm (Mochi, $4.45), 40 mm (Lantern, $5.45) or 60 mm (Wheelie, $6.75). Not sold on Amazon in white.
  • The charger + 5 V boost board (Adafruit, $8.95; also DigiKey) and one P-channel MOSFET for the lidar’s power switch.
  • Mochi alone adds the 1.28" round screen (about $15).

The colour rule, applied. The skins are ivory card sprayed canvas white, Pip’s white; the brass rods show only as the edges of the body, two hoops and two rails at most; black is allowed only where it cannot be helped: the lidar’s body, the screen’s glass, the camera’s lens, the tyres. No board shows anywhere. The wheels come white; spray their faces canvas white with the tyre masked if you want them to match the skin exactly.

A

Mochi: a loaf with a hat

A soft ivory loaf, 56 mm square in plan and 55 tall, with brass edges, one big round face and a black hat that spins. The wheels are small and tucked under, so it glides more than it rolls. It is the most compact of the three and the simplest frame: two hoops and two rails.

72 W × 56 D × 94 H mm (body 56 × 56 × 55, hat 33 above it) · wheels Ø31 at axle 16 · clearance 5 · about 212 g · lidar plane 86 mm up

Why these numbers

  • 56 wide is two motors end to end (24 + 2 + 24) and the card. 56 deep is the cell across the back, the motors, and the screen with the XIAO behind it.
  • Ø31 wheels keep the axle at 16 and the body low, so the hat does not make it tall: 94 over everything.
  • The lidar’s flange is wider than its body (54 × 46 mm), so it hides under the top skin on two cross rods and only the round 33 mm body shows, 3 mm behind centre.
  • The round screen’s board is Ø37.5, so the front needs a 40 mm flat zone: corner radius 6, not 10.
  • The cell sits across the back above the motors’ tails: the centre of mass lands 4 mm behind the axle, on the tail skid; the nose skid rides 1 mm up and catches a hard stop.

The trade

  • The smallest and the softest shape; the hat is the custom spin in one glance.
  • Fewest rods, one card wrap, flat panels front and back.
  • Lidar plane clear through 360°.
  • Its plane is 86 mm up: it sees mugs, hands, laptop lids and monitor feet, not books, phones or pens. The edge ranger, the camera and the bump sensor cover the low desk.
  • The round screen is a purchase; the owned square screen fits the same front with a 38 mm flat zone if you would rather not buy it.
B

Lantern: a head floating over the lidar

Two ivory blocks with brass edges and the lidar’s black rotor turning in the open band between them, like a lantern. The puck holds the heavy parts, the head holds the face, and three thin brass posts carry the head. It is the tallest, and the one that sees best.

Why these numbers

  • The puck is 27 tall: the motors at axle 19 pass under the lidar’s flange at 25, so the lidar can sit 7 mm down into the puck.
  • The band is the lidar’s own height above the puck plus 2 mm; the head is 44 tall because the owned 36 mm screen wants the lens above it.
  • Ø40 wheels at the back of the puck sit flush with its back face; the cell lies ahead of them, under the lidar.
  • Three 1.5 mm posts (two front corners, one back centre) hide 2–3° of the scan each; the loom of motor wires, cell leads and the I²C chain runs down the back post.
  • The centre of mass is 9 mm ahead of the axle and 42 mm up: it rests on its nose skid and tips backward only above 2 m/s², which the firmware never asks for.

The trade

  • The best lidar view of the three: 52 mm up, all round, 360° but three slivers.
  • Uses the owned square screen; nothing to buy for the face.
  • The heavy parts are low in the puck.
  • Tallest at 106 mm.
  • Every wire between puck and head runs down a post; the loom must be neat or it shows.
  • Two storeys read more ‘robot’ than ‘cute’; the proportion depends on the band staying black and empty.

What they share: the electronics

One cell, one boost, one brain. The cell feeds the motor driver straight (the motors never load the boost) and the charger-boost board, which makes the 5 V rail for the XIAO, the lidar and the amplifier and charges the cell at 500 mA over its own USB-C while the robot keeps running. The XIAO’s 5V pin takes the rail through a Schottky, as Seeed’s wiki asks, so the robot can be flashed over the XIAO’s USB-C with the toggle off and the PC never back-feeds the rail. The lidar gets its 5 V through a small MOSFET switch the firmware closes a second after boot, because the boost chip stalls if 300 mA is hanging on it at power-up (Adafruit’s note); the same switch is the lidar’s sleep.

What I need from you

Pick one letter, or a mix (Mochi’s body with Wheelie’s eyes is a legal combination; Lantern’s band on Mochi’s loaf is not, the motors are in the way). My pick is A, Mochi: it is the most compact, the softest shape, the fewest rods, and the hat says “custom” before the robot moves. Its one real cost is a high lidar plane, and the edge ranger, the camera and the bump sensor already cover the low desk. If the big ivory wheels matter more than compactness, C.

Three small decisions ride along: the round screen for Mochi (buy, about $15) or the owned square one; the wheels left white or sprayed canvas white; and whether the lidar goes in now or the owned 8×8 ranger stands in for a first, 35 mm shorter version.

Then step one finishes the family way: the chosen body redrawn at full detail with every part placed, the wiring sheet pin to pin at about 95 % confidence, and the order list in Amazon, DigiKey, Adafruit order. Education pages and firmware wait for the approved diagram, as the house rule says.
