Mochi: the loaf with a hat, drawn to build

Picked on 2026-11-07 from the three prototypes. An ivory card loaf inside two brass hoops, 77 × 56 × 93 mm, about 239 g: a round colour face, a camera on the brow, a laser ranger beside it for the desk edge, the lidar worn as a hat, a speaker in the floor, the microphone on the controller, two gear motors turning Crumb’s 64-tooth steel gears, faced in ivory, and the cell swapping through a hatch in the back. Every part placed to size, every wire pin to pin, every rod with a length. Crumb’s parts throughout; four things to buy.

What it is

Two hoops of 1.5 mm brass rod, 56 × 55 mm with r 4 corners, stand 56 mm apart; a strip of cardstock sprayed canvas white wraps round inside them and two discs close the sides, so the brass shows only as the body’s edges. Between the hoops, 1.0 mm rods carry everything: a floor rail and a keel with two brass domes under them, two cradles for the motors, a shelf for the cell holder, two cross rods for the lidar’s flange and one for the screen. The XIAO ESP32-S3 Sense stands behind the screen with its lens on the brow; the ranger looks down beside it; the motor driver stands on the right wall, the amplifier on the left, the fuel gauge and the speaker lie on the floor, and over the cell the motion sensor, the lidar’s switch and the charger-boost stack up, its USB-C out the back between the button and the toggle.

Black only in the lidar’s body, the screen’s glass and the lens; steel only as the gears’ toothed rims, 1.35 mm deep. No board shows. Brass only as lines: the two hoops, the floor rail, the brim ring round the hat, and the hub ends in the wheel faces.

Every wire, pin to pin

One cell, one toggle, one boost, one brain. The cell rail (red) runs through the fuel gauge into the charger-boost and straight to the motor driver. The 5 V rail (brass) comes off the charger-boost’s terminal block and feeds the XIAO through a Schottky, the amplifier, and the lidar through a switch the firmware closes a second after boot. Everything else is 3.3 V from the XIAO and four buses: SPI to the screen, I²S to the amp, UART from the lidar, and one I²C chain through the gauge, the driver, the ranger and the motion sensor.

From the cell: about 1.0 A roaming with the lidar on, so close to an hour; the lidar spinning only while it moves, about 1.5 h; parked with the face on and the lidar and camera off, 0.2 A, four to five hours. A full charge at 500 mA takes about 2.2 h, inside the charger’s six-hour timer, and the robot may keep running on the lead. The cell’s 2 A protection is the last resort: the firmware ramps drive and caps it at the level the stall test sets, so two stalled motors stay under 1.4 A.

Speed: about 60 rpm at the cell’s voltage, 45 under load, 9 cm/s on Ø40 wheels; the firmware drives slower than that on a desk. The Ø40 gear gives the motor 23 % less push at the rim than Rev A’s Ø31, which the N20 has to spare: 239 g at 0.4 m/s² is about 2 mN·m across both wheels, a hundredth of one N20’s stall.

What the firmware needs

  • Boot order: Wi-Fi up, screen on, chain scanned (0x36, 0x5D, 0x29, 0x68), then D2 high for the lidar, then the lidar parser on UART1 at 230400 (LDS library or the STL-19P packet: 0x54, 12 points, CRC8).
  • Safety loop at 50 Hz: ranger over 90 mm or no target → brake, back 15 mm, turn; motion sensor pitch or roll over 5° in 50 ms → brake; lidar closer than 80 mm ahead → slow, turn.
  • Drive: ramps of 0.4 m/s² (0.6 lifts the tail), the cap from the stall test, turns in place, reversing limited to 15 mm because nothing looks back.
  • Face: Pip’s eyes at 240 × 240 on the round screen, backlight PWM with a night floor and a parked-off state. Sound on I²S1, cap 50. Double tap on the body = the button.
  • Sleep: lidar switch off, camera off, Wi-Fi on; the gauge’s state of charge on the face.

Before power, with the meter

CheckHow
Holder and toggleCell in, nothing else connected: meter DC V across the toggle's outer lug and the holder's − lead, toggle on: +3.6 to +4.2 V. Negative means the leads are swapped; fix it at the holder.
Gauge pass-throughCell through the gauge, toggle on: the same voltage on the second JST, + on the pin that goes to the charger's BAT +. Generic pigtails are often wired opposite to Adafruit's convention: meter, then crimp.
Charger-boost aloneOnly the cell on BAT. Terminal block: 5.0–5.2 V between + and −. The charge-rate jumper on the back is cut (500 mA). EN left open.
No shorts, unpoweredToggle off, cell out. Ω: 5V rail to GND and BAT+ to GND above 1 kΩ and climbing (the capacitors); 3V3 to GND above 1 kΩ; 5V to BAT+ open.
Lidar switchToggle on, lidar unplugged: P-MOSFET drain reads 0 V. Short the switch's input to 3V3: drain reads the rail. Release: 0 V again. Then plug the lidar in and repeat; it spins up within a second of the input going high.
Schottky directionRail on, USB out: XIAO 5V pin reads rail minus 0.3 V. Toggle off, USB in: the XIAO boots and the rail reads 0 V (the diode blocks).
Screen and lensBefore the skin closes: a frame on the screen at 26 MHz, backlight PWM from 5 % to 100 %; the camera demo shows the desk ahead with the lens tilted 25° up; the ranger reads the desk at 50–90 mm ahead from its brow slot.
Edge test, wheels off the deskDrive loop running, a board under the ranger: pull it away and both motors stop within one 20 ms tick; tip the body 5° and the motion sensor stops them too.
Current, the real numbersEverything on the cell: idle with the face on, streaming, driving, lidar on. Log them next to the budget table; the run-time claims stand or fall on them.

Build order

  1. Bench tests on jumpers first: the chain scan (0x36, 0x5D, 0x29, 0x68), the screen, the lidar packets on D3, the switch, the amp, the motors from the bench supply at 3.7 V with a 0.5 A limit (stall current sets the drive cap).
  2. Bend the two hoops on the paper template (56 × 55, r 4); solder each closed with a 6 mm lap. Solder the floor rail and the keel between them; the two cradle Us; the shelf; the two flange Us; W1 for the screen.
  3. Cut the card: the wrap 209 × 54.5 with its openings, the two discs with their Ø8 shaft holes, two Ø36 face discs for the gears. Spray canvas white, two coats, dry overnight. Clear tape over the faces fingers touch.
  4. Hubs: 9 mm of 5 mm tube over 9 mm of 4 mm tube, soldered, one pressed into each gear with the boss inboard; spin each gear on a motor shaft and true it (1 mm of wobble at the rim is a waddle). Motors on the cradles, shafts out through the discs; gears on, the inner tube squeezed into the shaft's flat; ivory faces last. The body must sit level on both domes with both gears touching.
  5. Holder on the shelf, opening back; motion sensor, switch board and charger-boost stacked on it; toggle and button on the back; driver on the right wall; amp on the left; gauge and speaker on the floor.
  6. Face: screen on W1, XIAO behind it, camera module and ranger inside the top-front rounding. Lidar flange on its cross rods through the top skin's hole; brim ring over the join.
  7. Wire to the sheet; Kapton on every board back toward brass; meter every rod against every net; then the checks above in order.
  8. First roll on a towel on the floor, not the desk.

Risks

RiskWhat is done about it
The 1 A boost against the real drawTypical 560 mA, peaks near 950 with the amp loud and the lidar spinning up. If the rail dips, the fix is in firmware first (amp cap, lidar start after Wi-Fi is up); the fallback board is the bq24074 charger with a Pololu U3V16F5 step-up.
The lidar's flangeIts 54 × 46 mm plate is read from the LD19 drawing; the STL-19P shares the housing. Caliper it on arrival: the cross rods move with it, and the top skin's hole is cut last.
A high lidar plane86 mm up: it sees mugs, hands, laptop lids and monitor feet, not books or phones. The ranger, the camera and the bump sensor own the low desk.
Wi-Fi inside card and brassTwo hoops are not a cage; the antenna flex goes flat on the inside of the top skin at the front. Compare RSSI against the bare board before closing up.
Weight at the top45 g of lidar at 76 mm puts the centre of mass 37 mm up and 2.3 mm behind the axle: the tail dome carries about 22 g and the nose dome, 1 mm up, bounds any pitch. Ramp drive at 0.4 m/s² (0.6 lifts the tail); the only hard stop is the edge stop, which pitches it onto the nose dome. The gears put 59 g more on the axle, which is why it moved 1 mm forward: left at z 0 the tail would lift at 0.44.
No buttonEleven pins, eleven jobs. A double tap on the body, read by the motion sensor, is the button; the toggle is the only switch.
Card as a shellCardstock sprayed on both sides and sealed at the edges stays flat; it will dent if dropped. The brass carries every load; the card carries only itself, the camera module and the ranger.
Gear on hub on shaftThree fits in series, and Crumb's recipe wanted a drop of CA, which the bench does not have. The 4 mm tube's bore is the shaft's 3 mm: press it on and squeeze a dimple into the D-flat with pliers so it drives; the 5 mm tube soldered over it fills the gear's bore, and the boss's grub screw, if the gear has one, clamps the stack (else a bead of hot glue on the inboard end). True each wheel on its motor before the hoops close.
Steel teeth on the desk64 tooth tips a turn tick at about 48 a second at speed, faint on wood, sharper on glass, and they can mark a soft finish. Grip is not in question: 0.4 m/s² needs a friction coefficient of 0.04. A desk mat, or a silicone O-ring stretched over the teeth, if the tick matters; the look says bare teeth.
The seller's Ø40The gear is drawn from the listing (Ø40, 9 over the boss, 5 mm bore; 39.6 by its module) and it is on the bench: caliper it before anything is cut. The axle height is its radius plus 0.5; the width is 2 × (29.5 + its length over the boss).
