Three questions

A Raspberry Pi 5 on the drone?

No. The board alone is 46 g and wants 5 V at up to 5 A, so the aircraft to carry it starts near 300 g with bigger cells and 3-inch props: a different project. Even a Pi Zero 2 W (10 g) drags in a 5 V supply and a camera, about 25 g, and the drone grows into the 716-motor class. None of it is needed: the Wi-Fi link already exists for control, and a video stream rides the same link to the computer on the desk, which has more compute than any flying Pi.

The LiDAR?

The drone has no scanning LiDAR and never did: the payload plan rejected the 47 g LD19 that Crumb uses and the 110 g RPLIDAR. What it has are two laser rangers the size of a fingernail, 0.5 g each. The down-looking one gives one distance and is what height hold runs on; it is not optional for indoor flying. The forward one gives an 8 × 8 grid of distances over 60° to 3.5 m: a stop-before-the-wall reflex, not a map. Leaving it off saves 0.5 g and the biggest single firmware item (porting ST's driver, the boot re-addressing dance). It changes nothing in size.

Smaller drone, local processing?

The drone stays exactly this size; following adds nothing to it. Every added gram of thinking goes on the desk. Processing is local in the strict sense: the PC joins the drone's own Wi-Fi network, as the phone app does today, and no frame or command touches the internet.

What it can do, honestly

  • Rotate toward you. Yes. Yaw in place from the four motors, up to about 90° a second, driven by how far your box sits off the centre of the picture.
  • Keep its distance. Yes. Your height in the picture is the range: at 2.5 m a standing adult fills about two thirds of the frame. The controller holds 2.5 m, moving at up to 0.8 m/s.
  • Hold height. Yes, from the down-looking ranger: 1.2 m, about head height when you are seated. Not sensed by the camera at all.
  • Follow around a corner. Yes. It turns with you, and when it cuts the corner the forward ranger sees the wall the camera is not looking at and clamps forward speed to zero under 60 cm. That reflex runs on the drone, with or without the brain.
  • Hold still when it loses you. Not on its own. With no optical-flow sensor it has no horizontal position hold, so it hovers level but drifts. The rule: no person for 1 s, hover; 5 s, land. The controller's own 500 ms link timeout sits under that.
  • Run for how long. About 2.6 min a pack with the speaker fitted (3 min before it), four packs, so about 10 min of following per charge round. The limit of this class, not of the brain.

The forward ranger stays

Leaving it off was weighed: 0.5 g lighter, a symmetric nose with the flashlight on the centre line, two firmware items instead of four. The builder kept it on 2026-11-09, and following is the reason: the camera looks at the person, not at the wall the drone is about to cut past, and the ranger's stop-before-the-wall reflex covers exactly that blind spot. The nose stays as approved: flashlight left, ranger right.

What the decision costs. The two bigger firmware items stay on the list: ST's multizone driver ported to ESP-Drone's I²C layer, and the boot re-addressing of the down-looking ranger. Both were already in the payload plan; following adds nothing to them.

What is not promised

  • Position hold. No optical-flow sensor fits: the PMW3901 needs four SPI pins and the XIAO has none free with the ranger's LPn line in use. Expect slow drift and a slow orbit around you; the follow loop corrects aim and range, not sideslip.
  • Frame rate. 10 to 15 frames a second at 320 × 240 is the plan; the payload page's Wi-Fi budget is the open risk and is measured before the rate goes up. Following works at 5 fps, just less smoothly.
  • Light. The detector needs a lit room; the flashlight is for seeing, not for the detector.
  • Mapping. None. Room maps are Crumb's and the FamilyBot's job, with a real LiDAR on a ground robot that can carry it.
