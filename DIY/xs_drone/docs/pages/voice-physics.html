Why it hears for free and talks through one pin

The working behind the microphone and the speaker added to the drone on 2026-11-09 and approved that night: a microphone that speaks in single bits, a sigma-delta modulator on the last free pin, a divider that is also the filter, a class-D bridge on the flight pack, a sealed speaker on the tail, what the grams cost, and the limit the propellers set. Read after XS Drone Custom Build; the link it talks over is on XS Drone Following. Written 2026-11-10. Nothing measured yet: every figure is derived from the datasheets and the build page's numbers, and the two waveforms are simulated. Source of truth: xs_drone/docs/voice-physics.md.

The whole thing in one breath

The drone already had a microphone: a grain of a MEMS part on the camera board, wired inside the controller, that sends sound as single bits a couple of million times a second. The speaker is the new part, and it had one pin to work with. So the controller makes sound the same way the microphone hears it, as a stream of single bits a million times a second, a resistor and a capacitor turn the stream back into a voltage, a class-D amplifier the size of a thumbnail multiplies it by 16 straight from the flight pack, and a sealed speaker the size of a postage stamp on the tail turns it into about 70 dB at a metre.

The price is 4.0 g, which takes thrust to weight from 2.03 to 1.81, under the 2.0 gate the build set itself and inside the 1.5 floor the hover test allows. The limit is the propellers, not the parts: in flight they are louder than anything the drone could say or hear. This page takes each piece in the order the signal does and shows the working behind the numbers on the build page.

Hearing for free: a microphone that speaks in single bits

A MEMS microphone is a capacitor whose one plate is a diaphragm about a millimetre across, etched into silicon. Sound moves the plate, the capacitance changes, and an amplifier on the same chip turns that into a voltage. This one, a MEMSensing MSM261D3526H1CPM in a 3.5 × 2.65 × 0.94 mm can with a 0.33 mm hole in its lid, goes one step further: it carries its own sigma-delta modulator and sends out a pulse-density stream, one bit per clock, in which a louder sound is a denser run of ones. Its datasheet gives it -26 dBFS at 94 dB SPL, so it clips at 120 dB SPL, the level of a chainsaw at arm's length; a signal-to-noise ratio of 64 dB(A), so its own hiss sits at the equivalent of 30 dB SPL, a quiet library; and it takes 0.67 mA.

It costs nothing because Seeed put it on the Sense board next to the camera, facing up through the board, and wired its clock and data to GPIO42 and GPIO41 through the board-to-board connector; neither pin reaches a castellation, so the drone's pin budget never saw them. The ESP32-S3's first I2S controller has a hardware filter that takes the one-bit stream in, decimates it to 16-bit samples and writes them to memory by DMA, so the flight controller never touches a sample until a 16 kHz buffer is ready.

One number on that path wants attention. The ESP-IDF driver runs the microphone's clock at 64 times the sample rate by default, which at 16 kHz is 1.024 MHz. The microphone's datasheet lists a low-power mode from 150 to 900 kHz and a standard mode from 1.1 to 4.0 MHz, and 1.024 MHz falls in the gap between them. Seeed's own example runs there and calls it stable, and it probably is, but it is off the sheet. Asking the driver for the 128× ratio instead puts the clock at 2.048 MHz, squarely in the standard band, for the same 16 kHz samples and one line of configuration. The microphone task will.

Speech from a metre away is about 60 dB SPL at the microphone, -60 dB below full scale and 30 dB above its floor: a clean recording in a quiet room. The 16-bit samples go up the Wi-Fi link to the desk computer, which does the listening; the drone keeps none of it.

One pin left, so the speaker goes analog

The XIAO brings eleven pins to its castellations and the drone uses ten: four motors, the pack divider, two for the I²C bus, the lamp, the forward ranger's enable and the motion sensor's interrupt. D10, GPIO9, is the last one. The owned I²S amplifiers need three pins (clock, word select, data), so they are out; the payload plan said so in October and parked a piezo beeper across the flashlight's LED as the only sound the drone would make. One pin can still make a voice, if it goes analog.

The ESP32-S3 has no analog output pin, but it has eight sigma-delta modulators that can drive any GPIO, and Espressif documents them as an 8-bit DAC. Each one takes a density, a signed byte from −128 to 127, and produces a pulse train at a clock of your choosing whose fraction of high pulses is (density + 128)/256; the voltage a low-pass filter recovers is VDD/256 × density + VDD/2, so density 0 is a steady 1.65 V and a sine of densities is a sine of volts riding on it. The modulator on this chip is second-order: the error it makes in each pulse is fed back twice over, which pushes the quantisation noise upward in frequency, away from the sound and toward the clock, where a filter can take it off.

The firmware feeds it from a ring buffer of samples. A hardware timer fires 16 000 times a second and its interrupt writes the next sample into the density register: one register write every 62.5 µs, about 1.6 % of one core at a microsecond each, and no DMA, because the modulator has no DMA path. Espressif's own example does exactly this with a 10 kHz timer and a sine table. 1 MHz is the pulse rate the documentation uses in its own example and 62 times the 8 kHz the samples can carry; a second-order modulator at that oversampling keeps its own noise about 85 dB below a full-scale tone, while eight bits of density can do no better than 50 dB. The knob, not the modulator, sets the floor, and 50 dB is telephone grade, which is what a sealed 20 mm speaker can use. A faster clock would buy nothing audible.

Espressif adds that densities between −90 and 90 give better randomness, and that is the second-order modulator talking: driven close to full scale it falls into short repeating patterns, idle tones, that a filter cannot remove. The firmware's everyday level is ±64, 6 dB under full scale, inside that range with room to spare; full scale is for a shout. The simulation above runs the same modulator and stays well behaved at ±64. Why sigma-delta and not plain PWM from the LED controller, which could also make eight bits at 300 kHz? Both would work. The modulator is the peripheral Espressif built and documents for this, it shapes its noise upward instead of leaving it at one carrier, and it costs the same one pin.

A divider that is also the filter

The pin swings 3.3 V and the amplifier wants a fraction of a volt, so the first job of the two resistors is to divide. 10 kΩ over 1.5 kΩ is a ratio of 7.7; with the amplifier's own 18 kΩ input resistor hanging across the lower leg it is 8.2, which turns a full-scale sine of densities, 3.27 V peak to peak at the pin, into 0.40 V at the node (0.43 with nothing connected, the figure on the build page). The everyday level is 0.20 V. The 201 mV of standing voltage from the density-zero midpoint goes no further than the amplifier's input capacitor.

The 22 nF across the lower leg is the second job. Seen from the capacitor the three resistors in parallel are 1216 Ω, and 1216 Ω into 22 nF is a corner at 5.9 kHz (5.5 kHz with the amplifier disconnected). Below the corner the node follows the density; above it the response falls 20 dB for every tenfold in frequency. The voice band, 300 to 3 400 Hz, loses at most 1.2 dB at its top. The pulses at 1 MHz are 45 dB down, 2.4 mV of ripple on a 398 mV signal, and the amplifier's switching at 260 kHz would be 33 dB down if it ever came back that way. What a first-order filter leaves is the modulator's shaped noise between about 20 and 200 kHz, the fuzz on the simulated trace in the figure above: above the speaker's range and far above hearing.

A first-order filter is gentle, and the one thing it does not finish is the stair-steps. The timer holds each sample for 62.5 µs, so a 1 kHz tone also carries faint images at 15 and 17 kHz; the hold itself attenuates them by about 24 dB and the filter by another 9, about 33 dB in all, a whistle at the edge of adult hearing that most ears will not find. Speech has little above 4 kHz to make images from. Test C11 listens for it anyway.

Espressif recommends an active filter after the modulator, for isolation and so as not to lose voltage. Here the isolation exists already: the amplifier's 18 kΩ input is 15 times the node's 1216 Ω, so it loads the divider by 7 %, and losing voltage is the point, since 3.3 V into a 24 dB amplifier would be 52 V of demand on a 3.7 V pack. Three passive parts from the owned kits, half a gram with the reservoir, do the job an op-amp board would do at a gram and a rail.

Twenty-four decibels and a bridge: the class-D amplifier

A class-D amplifier does not amplify the waveform; it switches. The PAM8403 compares the input with a triangle wave and drives its output transistors fully on or fully off at 260 kHz, with the on-time proportional to the signal. The speaker's own inductance and the ear do the averaging, the transistors are never half on, and almost no power is lost in them: the datasheet gives up to 90 %, 83 % at a 4 Ω load driven hard. At the full 0.62 W into this speaker the chip would warm by about 110 mW; a linear amplifier of the old kind would throw away as much as it delivered and want a heatsink, a bigger board and, for this swing, a higher rail.

Its gain is fixed at 24 dB, 15.8 times, which is why the divider is there. Its two outputs for one channel swing in opposite directions about half the supply, so the speaker between them sees twice the swing of either and four times the power that one output into ground could give: a bridge. On a 3.7 V pack, after the 320 mΩ of the two switches in the path, that is 7.1 V peak to peak at most. Full scale asks for 6.3 V and needs a pack above 3.28 V; once the pack sags toward 3.3 V under a hover the ceiling is 6.3 V and full scale would clip. The everyday level asks for 3.2 V and needs 1.65 V, which is less than the chip's own 2.0 V cut-out, so it never clips on any pack the drone will fly. The datasheet puts 0.72 W into 8 Ω at 3.6 V and 1 % distortion; full scale here is 0.62 W, under the speaker's 0.8 W rating, which at 8 Ω is itself 7.2 V peak to peak, the same ceiling the pack sets.

Neither output is ground. OUT L− is a live amplifier output sitting at half the supply, and tying it to the frame would short one half of the bridge; the chip would detect it and mute, and at best the speaker would get half the voltage and a quarter of the power. The right channel's input and both of its outputs stay open. The board has no shutdown pad, so the amplifier is always on at 10 mA, 0.2 % of a hover and 20 hours of a pack on the ground; and at power-up its bypass capacitor charges through the input stage and the speaker gives a soft pop, which the datasheet says a shutdown pin would have hidden. The drone will click when it wakes.

It runs from VBAT, the pack itself, because its range is 2.5 to 5.5 V and because the XIAO's 3.3 V regulator already feeds the controller, the camera and three sensors and should not also supply the 198 mA average and 394 mA crests of a loud sentence. Those crests come in bursts at the audio frequency and at 260 kHz; the pack's leads cannot deliver them instantly, so a 47 to 100 µF capacitor across the amplifier's power pads holds the rail through each burst, beside the 1 µF ceramic the board carries. The datasheet asks for a bulk capacitor of at least 20 µF for this reason. The same reservoir keeps the amplifier's bursts off the divider that the flight controller reads the pack voltage from.

A sealed box five millimetres thick: the speaker

A bare speaker element in the open is nearly silent at low frequencies: the back of the cone pushes air out while the front pulls it in, and round the edge the two cancel. A box behind the cone stops that. The Same Sky part is such a box, 20 × 13 mm and 4.9 mm thick in ABS, with a PEEK cone and a neodymium magnet, and its sealed back is why it was chosen over the 0.5 g bare element inside it: on a wire frame with nothing to mount it to, the bare element would have had no baffle and no bass at all.

The datasheet gives a resonance at 1.3 kHz and a response from 0.5 to 20 kHz. Below resonance a sealed speaker falls 12 dB for every halving of frequency: 650 Hz is -12 dB down, 325 Hz is -24. The fundamentals of a voice, 100 to 250 Hz, do not come out of it. They do not need to: the ear hears a pitch in the harmonics that remain, which is how a telephone has always worked, and the words live in the band from 300 to 3 400 Hz that the speaker, the filter and the 8-bit converter all pass. The chain is telephone grade from end to end, and not by accident.

Its 8 Ω is the load the amplifier's table is written for, and its 0.8 W rating (1.0 W for a second at a time) is above the 0.62 W that full scale can ask. It is omnidirectional through the voice band: at 1 kHz the wavelength is 343 mm against a 13 mm cone, and the sound only begins to beam above 8 kHz. So grille up on the tail costs no loudness in any direction a listener could be; it keeps the grille out of the straps and away from the pan, which would otherwise make a second box in front of the first.

The datasheet's 91 dB is at 0.8 W and 10 cm. Power counts in tenths: 0.62 W is -1.1 dB down, 84 dB at 10 cm for the everyday level. Distance counts in squares: every doubling takes 6 dB, so 1 m is 20 dB under 10 cm. That makes 70 dB at a metre at full scale and 64 at the everyday level, against about 60 dB for a person talking to you from the same distance: a clear voice across a desk, a quiet one across a room, 60 dB at 3 m.

Where it sits and what it weighs

The speaker lies on the tail of the floor pan, 10.5 to 30.5 mm behind the centre, and the amplifier hangs under the hood at the nose, 11 to 29.5 mm ahead of it. Two grams at -20.5 mm and one and a half at +20.2 mm move the aircraft's centre of gravity 0.28 mm toward the tail, and with the motors 30 mm either side of the centre the rear pair carries 50.5 % of the hover instead of 50. The controller trims that without being told. The speaker's top at 10.9 mm is 5.1 mm under the propeller plane, and the amplifier's components face the floor with 1.5 mm of air on the skids, 0.5 mm short of the front axle.

Two straps of the clear tape hold the speaker to the pack, and a loop of 30 AWG through each flange hole ties it to the rails. In a full-throttle punch the speaker weighs about 4 gf; the tape alone holds that many times over, and the wire loops are there for the day the adhesive warms in the sun and lets go. Nothing is glued, painted or covered: the housing is black, one of the family's colours, and the amplifier's blue board is out of sight under the hood.

The grams are the real cost. A hovering rotor's power goes as the thrust to the power 1.5, so the hover current scales the same way with mass: 2.4 A at 25 g becomes 3.7 A at 33.5 g and 4.4 A at 37.5 g, 18 C from a 30 C pack. Thrust to weight on the vendor's 68 gf falls from 2.03 to 1.81, and since a brushed motor's thrust rises about as the square of its duty, the hover command rises from 70 to 74 %. The gate the build set itself is 2.0, which the 33.5 g aircraft just clears at 34 g; the floor the hover test accepts is 1.5, which is 45 g. Eighty per cent of the pack over 4.4 A, less 5 % for the controller and the camera, is 2.6 minutes in the air against 3.1 before: the voice costs about 29 seconds a flight.

If the first hover reads under 1.8, the build document names the way back: the bare 0.5 g element, or the 716 motors already in the plan. The ledger's masses are estimates until the coin balance weighs them.

The honest limit: the propellers drown both ends

Nobody has published a sound-level measurement of a whoop this size. The nearest is an 80 g Tello, measured at 46 dB(A) from 13 m, which is about 68 dB(A) at 1 m; a 1 kg Phantom measures 70 to 80 dB(A) at 1.5 m. A 38 g aircraft on four 31 mm propellers at 30 000 rpm is likely in the 65 to 70 dB(A) range at 1 m, and the page treats that as an estimate.

The microphone sits about 3 cm from the nearest propeller, where that noise is 95 to 100 dB: under the microphone's 120 dB overload point, so it does not clip, but 35 to 40 dB above a voice speaking from a metre away. No recogniser works 35 dB under the noise, and the noise is tonal and in the voice band: the motors' fundamental is around 500 Hz with harmonics above it, and it also reaches the microphone through the frame, since the brushed motors shake the stainless rails the camera board stands on. In flight the drone cannot hear you. Commands in flight go through the desk computer's own microphone, which hears you fine.

The speaker's 70 dB at 1 m is a few decibels under the propellers' 65 to 70, and both fall together with distance, so in flight a sentence is audible as a voice under the whine at arm's length and lost beyond it. A tone or a chime cuts through better than words. So the design is for a drone that announces while flying and converses when it has landed, perched on the desk or in a hand: there the propellers are off, the microphone's floor is a library and the speaker is a voice across a desk.

Talking and listening at once is the other limit. The speaker is 24 mm from the microphone and at full scale puts about 102 dB SPL on it, well under its overload but far louder than a person in the room. The character server already takes turns, listening, then speaking, then listening again, and the firmware keeps the microphone stream muted while the speaker plays. There is no echo canceller on a flight controller, and none is needed if nobody talks over the drone.

Over the air: what a voice costs the link

The drone sends and receives plain 16-bit samples at 16 kHz: 256 kbit/s each way, 640 bytes in each 20 ms frame. The camera stream on the same link is 1.2 to 1.8 Mbit/s, so the voice adds 14 to 21 % to it. Compressing it on the drone would save bandwidth nobody needs and cost the controller work it does need for the attitude loop; the character server already speaks this protocol in PCM, the same one Pip uses, so the drone is a wire and the desk computer does the listening, the thinking and the talking.

The delay is the Wi-Fi hop, 10 to 30 ms each way, plus the 20 ms frame: well under a tenth of a second before the server starts to think. Speech recognition, the reply and the voice synthesis take a second or two on the desk machine, and that, not the drone, is the pause the builder will hear.

Which part each idea lives in

  • single-bit hearing, 120 dB of overload, a clock that should be 2.05 MHz: XIAO ESP32-S3 Sense, whose camera board carries the microphone
  • eight bits of density a million times a second, on the one free pin: the same controller's sigma-delta modulator on GPIO9
  • divide by 8.2 and roll off at 6 kHz: 10 kΩ and 1.5 kΩ from the owned resistor kit
  • the capacitor that averages the pulses: 22 nF from the owned ceramic kit
  • the reservoir that holds the rail through a loud word: 47 to 100 µF from the owned electrolytic kit
  • 24 dB, a bridge, switching at 260 kHz: PAM8403 class-D amplifier board, a 5-pack, the version without the knob, the one line to buy
  • a sealed box, 1.3 kHz resonance, 91 dB at 10 cm: Same Sky enclosed speaker, one of Pip's two spares
  • the straps and the ties: the clear heat tape bought 2026-11-07 and the owned 30 AWG wire

What comes next

The amplifier 5-pack is in the builder's Amazon cart and not yet ordered; everything else is on hand. At build step 1, with the pack on the bench, test C11: a 1 kHz tone from the modulator should read about 0.43 V peak to peak at the node with the amplifier disconnected and about 0.40 V with it connected, play clean, and show under 20 mA of quiescent draw (the datasheet's typical is 10 mA at 3.6 V). At step 6, test C12: props off, a spoken sentence transcribed on the desk server and its reply heard at 1 m, both over the drone's own Wi-Fi with the camera running. Firmware: the microphone task asks the I2S driver for the 128× ratio, so the clock is 2.05 MHz; the playback task keeps the everyday level at ±64 and mutes the microphone while it plays. If an idle tone is audible at any level, the fix is in software: keep the density within ±90 and, if that is not enough, raise the pulse rate. The first measurements replace this page's estimates: the mass on the coin balance, the hover command, the voltage at the node, the sound at 1 m if a meter is ever to hand.
