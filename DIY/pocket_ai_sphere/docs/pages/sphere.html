A Pocket AI in a brass sphere that looks at you

The same assistant as the first Pocket AI, plus a camera, a weather sensor and a head that follows you: the whole sphere nods and turns on a brass fork. Rev B was approved 2026-11-10; the same day the builder asked for the moving head, the motion was shown and approved, and Rev C, step one of the build rule, was approved as drawn on 2026-11-10. Nothing bent, soldered or flashed; one board in the mail; three lines to buy, listed in docs/ORDER.md. Source: pocket_ai_sphere/docs/DESIGN.md, drawn by docs/pages/gen/sphere.py.

Three rings, two posts, a fork. A vertical ring of 1.5 mm brass rod, a top ring and a base ring; two thin posts between the cap rings carry the screen. The vertical ring's right pole is a servo: its body is set into the ring, its shaft points out along the sphere's axis, and the fork holds its horn still, so the servo tilts the sphere around itself. The left pole is a brass stub in a bushing. The fork is three pieces of 4 mm brass tube on a second servo standing on a brass coaster, and that one turns the whole thing. Inside, the owned 1.54 inch colour screen fills the open front with the camera board behind it, lens over the top edge; the cell lies on the floor as ballast with the speaker on it; the charger-boost stands behind the tray; the sensor hangs under the top ring. The two blue parts stand behind the screen where nothing sees them; the chrome lever, the white button cap and white wire are the only things that are not brass or black.

What changed from Rev B

  • The whole sphere is the head. The builder asked for eyes that face where the camera faces and move to follow (2026-11-10). A pan-tilt gimbal inside does not fit: the screen with the camera board is 33 x 48 mm, a pan servo stands 21 mm under it and the tray takes 14, more than an 80 mm sphere, and the swept head needs clear space around it. So the sphere itself nods and turns, the screen and the camera rigid to each other, and nothing swings inside. Shown first on the motion page and approved.
  • The tilt servo is the right hinge. The vertical ring's two ends enter the servo's mounting ears at the right pole, its shaft points out along the sphere's axis, and the fork holds its horn still: the servo turns itself and the sphere around it. The left pole is a brass stub in a 3 mm bushing. No wire crosses the hinge.
  • A fork and a coaster. Two 4 mm brass tube tines and a bar, lapped and soldered, rise from a pan servo standing on the family's brass coaster. The three wires to that servo run inside the left tine. 105 mm tall on the desk.
  • The equator is gone; a top ring mirrors the base ring, as asked. The front is one open window from the base ring to the top ring.
  • The owned 1.54" colour screen is the face, the builder's pick: 28 mm of glass against the 0.96" OLED's 22 x 11, and the OLED's board was blue. The camera board sits behind its upper half with the lens over the top edge, so the eyes and the camera look the same way by construction; the pupils are drawn where the camera sees you.
  • A charger-boost comes in. The servos are rated 4.8-6 V and will not run on the cell, so the family's bq25185 board makes 5 V for them and the amp, and charges the cell at 500 mA, five times faster than the XIAO.
  • Two pins were let go. Eleven pins carry thirteen jobs, so the amp's shutdown line is left open (on, as the rover's) and the screen's backlight is tied on, undimmed. A Rev D could return either with an I2C expander.
  • The posts moved out to x = ±17, just outside the new screen's edges, running straight between the two cap rings, so no tie to any equator is needed and the screen glues to them by its edges.
  • Three lines to buy: two servos, the charger-boost and a coaster, about $60 landed. Rev B had none.

How it moves

Shown and approved on the motion page before this drawing. The camera sees about 66° across and 50° up and down. While you are inside that cone only the pupils move: the firmware draws them where it sees your face. Past about 18° off the sphere's axis the eyes are near the edge of the glass, and after a short pause the sphere turns to put you back in the middle while the eyes slide home. Pan stops at ±60° (the wires in the left tine twist no further), tilt at ±25°. The servos are rated 0.1 s per 60°; the firmware eases every move and never slews both from rest at once. With the power off the sphere hangs level on its own: its centre of mass sits 7 mm under the tilt axis.

Why these numbers

  • 70 mm still. Nothing swings inside now, so the sphere did not have to grow: the 33 x 36 mm screen sits 15 mm forward of the centre with its lower corners 1 mm inside the surface, and the camera board's top at y = 24 is 5 mm inside.
  • The hinge is the servo. Any horizontal axis through the centre passes through the vertical ring at the poles, so a servo inside could not reach a horn outside: its shaft would hit the ring. Setting the servo into the ring, with the ring's ends in its ears, puts the shaft on the axis with nothing in the way, and the ring's two ends hold it as well as a bracket would.
  • 0.022 kg-cm to tilt. 72 g with its centre of mass 7 mm under the axis: at 25° that is 0.022 kg-cm against the servo's 0.5. The pan servo turns about 80 g on its own bearing with no moment to hold; its plastic bushing takes the fork's weight, which is the one thing to watch.
  • 105 mm tall. Coaster 8, servo 17.3, horn 4, bar 4, 2 mm of clearance, then the 70 mm sphere: the sphere's centre is 70 mm above the desk, which puts the lens 90 mm up, about the height of a face across a desk when it tilts up.
  • The fork's tines at ±39.5 mm clear the sphere by 4.5 mm; the left one is a 4 mm tube because three wires run inside it. Tube in three pieces, lapped and soldered, because a 4 mm tube does not bend to a 10 mm radius without a mandrel.
  • A coaster that tips at 28° with 80 g under 90 g on a 70 mm lever; the satellite's answer, 150 g of steel in the hollow, makes it 45°. The washers are optional on day one.
  • 5 V from a boost, not from USB. The XIAO's 5V pin is dead on battery, and the servos want 4.8 V or more. The charger-boost already belongs to the family, costs $7 and charges five times faster into the bargain.
  • Eleven pins for thirteen jobs. Three for I2S, two for I2C, three for the screen, two servos, the button: eleven. The amp's shutdown line and backlight dimming went, as on the rover.

Carried over, and new
  • The cell is the only source. Nothing into the XIAO's 5V pin; only the holder on the BAT pads, through the toggle.
  • Volume cap 65; GAIN open. The amp's SD is open now: a soft click at power-up is possible; a Rev D expander gets it back if it matters.
  • Neither speaker lead is ground. No GND wire, no scope ground.
  • The brass is not a conductor: meter every ring, post and tine against every net before the cell goes in, and after every solder session.

Build order

  1. Bench-test every part first (the nine steps). Nothing goes near brass untested.
  2. Look at the screen's board in Crumb's box and note its colour; measure the servo's shaft offset and the horn's holes with the calipers.
  3. Bend the vertical ring and the two cap rings; lap-join the cap rings. Check them on 70 mm and 44 mm circles drawn with a compass.
  4. Glue the vertical ring's two ends into the tilt servo's ears, shaft pointing out: the hinge. Solder the top and base rings to the vertical ring at front and back, then the two posts between them at x = ±17. Harris flux, baking soda, IPA, as on the first frame. Solder the left stub at the left pole. Meter: one piece of metal, nothing else on it.
  5. Wire the head on the bench, in white: the screen's eight, the camera board's BAT pair, the button's two, the amp's five, the sensor's four, the two servo leads to 3-pin headers, the charger's JST and 5 V pair.
  6. Glue the camera board to the back of the screen with Kapton between, lens 5.5 mm over the top edge; glue the screen's side edges to the posts, 22 mm below the centre. Lay the wire bundle down behind the boards.
  7. Tape the toggle to the tray's front wall, lever out to the right, and the amp to the back of the screen below the camera board. Tray onto the base ring with two white ties; speaker across the tray's walls over the cell, grille up; the charger standing behind the tray, USB-C to the right, with the 220 µF; the sensor onto its hooks; the button under the top ring at the front, cap up.
  8. The fork: cut the two tines and the bar from the 4 mm tube, solder the bushing across the left tine's top, lap and solder the bar to both tines. Thread the three pan wires down inside the left tine first.
  9. Hang the sphere: the stub into the bushing, the pin through the right tine's top into the tilt horn's outer hole. Press the horn onto the servo with the sphere level and the servo at its middle pulse, then its screw.
  10. The stand: glue the pan servo standing on the coaster's centre, horn arm forward; glue the bar onto the arm with the keeper. Steel washers into the coaster's hollow, pad back on. Plug the three wires onto the pan servo's lead.
  11. Meter frame-to-every-net before the cell goes in, then after every solder session. First power-up on the charger's USB-C with the toggle ON and the servos unplugged, then the servos, then the cell alone. Run the test-9 sketch again on the fork, with the motion study's dead band and limits.

What could go wrong

  • The screen's board colour. Not recorded. If it is not black, its 8 mm tab shows under the glass. Look in Crumb's box before anything else; a black-board 1.54" is the Rev D answer.
  • The pan servo's bushing. A sub-micro servo's output shaft turns in plastic; 80 g on a 70 mm lever may wobble. If it does, a 9 g FS90 in the same place, or a 3 mm tube bearing round the horn.
  • The ring's ends in the servo's ears. Glue, not solder, holds the hinge to the frame. Rough the rod, fill the ear's hole, let it cure a day; if it ever creeps, a turn of 0.5 mm wire through the ear.
  • The camera flex and the microphone. The lens is 5.5 mm over the screen's edge, the mic port 3.5. If the mic port lands behind the screen on the real board, raise the camera board 2 mm.
  • The boost under both servos. 0.8 A if both stall; the firmware starts them apart and the 220 µF rides the steps.
  • Wi-Fi. The antenna lies flat on the XIAO's back under Kapton, behind the screen's board: test the range in step 9 before brass.
  • It is top-heavy on an 80 g coaster until the washers go in.
