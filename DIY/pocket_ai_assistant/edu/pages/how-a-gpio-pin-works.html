How does a GPIO pin work?

GPIO means general-purpose input/output. Each pin is a small doorway between A1's chip and the outside world, and it can do one of two jobs: push a voltage out, or read a voltage in. Inside, that is two switches, one sensor, two weak springs, and a switchboard.

Output

A1 decides the voltage. GPIO5 does this to switch the amplifier on and off.

Input

The outside world decides and A1 reads it. GPIO10 does this for your button.

Handed over

A built-in block such as I2S or I2C drives the pin for you. GPIO1 to GPIO4 and GPIO20/21 work this way.

1. What is inside one pin

Every pin on the ESP32-C3 has the same small circuit behind it. The chip's firmware flips tiny switches in this circuit by writing numbers into registers.

2. The same pin, as water

Think of the pin as a small pipe junction. A tank on the roof holds water at the 3.3 V level, and a drain below is at ground. The water level in the junction is the pin's voltage.

Two things never to do, in water terms:

  • Open both big valves. Water pours straight from the tank to the drain. In a chip that is a short from 3.3 V to GND, and the firmware prevents it.
  • Connect a taller tank. A 5 V source on a pin pushes water backwards over the protection diodes into the 3.3 V supply. That is why nothing above 3.3 V ever goes on an A1 GPIO.

3. How the pin decides 1 or 0

The input sensor does not measure exact voltages. It sorts them into three bands, from the ESP32-C3 datasheet: 1 above 75 % of the supply, 0 below 25 %, and no promise in between.

4. The switchboard, and your pins

The ESP32-C3 has built-in helpers that speak a protocol for you: I2S for audio, I2C for the display. A switchboard inside the chip, the GPIO matrix, plugs each helper's signal into whichever pin the firmware names in config.h. That is why the pin numbers are a choice, not a law.

Check yourself

Your button reads 1 when you are not touching it. Why?

GPIO10 is an input with its pull-up on. The thin trickle from 3.3 V holds the pin high until the pressed button connects it to GND, which wins easily.

Why did the amplifier's SD pin need your resistor, even though GPIO5 can drive it?

While A1 boots or resets, GPIO5 is not yet an output, so both of its big valves are shut and the pin floats. Your pull-down holds SD at 0, meaning off, until the firmware takes over.

Why must nothing above 3.3 V touch a GPIO?

The protection diodes connect the pin to the 3.3 V supply. A higher voltage pushes current backwards through them, which can damage the pin or lift the whole 3.3 V rail.
