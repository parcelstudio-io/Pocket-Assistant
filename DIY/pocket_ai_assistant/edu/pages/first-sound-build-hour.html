One hour: make A1 speak

Solder the amplifier, wire it to A1, and hear a reply come out of the speaker. On 22 September you chose to do this without the dummy load or the oscilloscope. The firmware's volume cap of 15 is what keeps the speaker safe, and the datasheet arithmetic below shows why it is enough.

Already done for you, 22 September

The speaking image is built and verified on your computer: amplifier enabled, volume cap 15, SHA-256 starting c98ee78f. The flash preview printed amplifier: ENABLED (Step 11, USB-powered). No compiling is needed in this hour unless you build something else first.

The shortcut you chose, and what protects the speaker

Step 11 as written sends the first sound into a dummy load and measures it with the scope before the speaker goes on. You decided to skip both for this prototype. What stands between the amplifier and the speaker is now the firmware's volume cap. The firmware clamps every volume change to 15, including one the assistant makes when you ask it to be louder.

Before you start
  • On the bench: A1 on its breadboard with its USB cable, one HiLetgo amplifier labelled AMP-A1 with its header strip and terminal block, and one speaker labelled SPK-A1.
  • From the kits: one 220 µF electrolytic rated 10 V or more, one 100 nF ceramic marked 104, one 4.7 kΩ resistor and one 1 kΩ in reserve. Confirm both resistors with the meter. You also need seven jumpers.
  • Tools: iron, solder, flux, wick, the multimeter, and your phone camera.
  • Rules: USB is the only power, and the cells and charger stay in another room. The amplifier's VIN comes only from A1's 5V pin. Neither speaker terminal ever touches GND. Unplug USB before every wiring change.

0:05Solder the headers

  1. Warm up. Set the iron to the temperature you used in Step 5 and make two practice joints on scrap header pins.

  2. Set up the jig. Push the header strip's long ends into the breadboard. Then set AMP-A1 over the short ends with its printed labels facing up.

  3. Solder the two end pins first, and check that the board sits flat before doing the five between them. Give each joint about two seconds of heat, then feed the solder in.

  4. Add the terminal block. Lift the board out and push the green terminal block in from the labelled side, with its wire openings facing outward. Tape it in place, turn the board over, and solder its two pins from underneath. They are bigger, so give them three or four seconds.

  5. Inspect at phone macro zoom. Every joint should be a shiny cone. Clear any bridge with flux and the wick, and reheat any ball with a touch of flux.

0:20Wire it, with one jumper left out

USB stays unplugged. Every connection except one goes in now. The jumper from GPIO5 to SD waits until 0:40, so that at first power the pull-down resistor alone is holding the amplifier off, and you can measure whether it manages.

  1. Place the amplifier on A1's breadboard with its header across the strips. Keep it away from A1's antenna end, the red ceramic block.

  2. Run five jumpers, all from A1's 5V edge: GPIO1 to LRC, GPIO2 to BCLK, GPIO3 to DIN, GND to GND, and A1's 5V pin to VIN. GPIO1 and GPIO2 already carry the microphone's wires, so the new jumper goes into the same strip as the existing one. Read 5V off the silkscreen; two pins further along the same edge is 3.3, which must not go to VIN.

  3. Add the 4.7 kΩ standing up, from the SD strip to the GND strip. Bend one leg down alongside the body.

  4. Add the 220 µF from the GND strip to the VIN strip, with the striped leg in GND. Put the 100 nF beside it; it goes in either way round. Check the stripe a second time.

  5. Leave both the GPIO5 jumper and the speaker terminals empty. Photograph the wiring from above.

0:32Three meter checks, still unplugged

0:35First power, amplifier held off

Plug USB in. A1 boots the assistant image it already runs, and that image never enables the amplifier. GPIO5 is not even connected yet, so two resistors decide where SD sits: the board's own pull-up to VIN, if it has one, and your 4.7 kΩ to GND.

  1. Plug USB in and let A1 boot. The OLED comes up as usual and the amplifier stays silent.

  2. Measure with the meter on DC volts, black probe on GND. Red on VIN should read close to 5 V. Red on SD must read below 0.08 V. Write both down.

  3. If SD reads 0.08 V or more, unplug USB, swap the 4.7 kΩ for the 1 kΩ, and measure again. If it is still 0.08 V or more, stop at the first diamond and photograph both sides of the board.

  4. Quick-press the button and ask "What is two plus two?" The reply should appear on the OLED as in Step 10. That shows the microphone and the shared clocks still work with the amplifier on their lines.

  5. Watch for a minute with no reset, then unplug USB.

0:40Flash the speaking image

  1. With USB unplugged, add the last jumper, from GPIO5 to row 2 of the SD strip. GPIO5 is on the edge opposite 5V, the pin nearest the USB socket on that side. Then plug USB back in.

  2. Open a terminal and check the image. Continue only if the verifier prints amplifier: ENABLED (Step 11, USB-powered).

    cd ~/Desktop/Projects/DIY/pocket_ai_assistant
. firmware/.work/esp-idf/export.sh
python3 firmware/scripts/verify_source_build.py

    If it prints disabled, something else was built since 22 September. Rebuild the speaking image first; it takes about five minutes.

    firmware/scripts/build.sh --assistant --amplifier

  3. Flash with the monitor open, keeping your Wi-Fi. --keep-wifi skips the settings area, so A1 remembers your network. Type FLASH /dev/ttyACM0 when it asks. If the port is different, ls /dev/ttyACM* lists it.

    firmware/scripts/flash.sh /dev/ttyACM0 --keep-wifi --monitor

  4. Find this line in the log. It proves the amplifier is enabled and the cap is 15.

    Prototype volume cap: 15; amplifier: qualified opt-in

0:45Wi-Fi again

Flashed with --keep-wifi? Skip this section: the log shows Connected to WiFi with your network within seconds, and you are at diamond C. On 24 September A1 rejoined its saved network this way. The rest of this section is for a plain flash, which writes a blank settings area, so A1 has forgotten your network. This is Step 10 again, and the 8.5 dBm cap that made the hotspot visible is still in the image. Do it somewhere private, because the setup hotspot is open.

  1. Watch the OLED for the hotspot name and http://192.168.4.1. The log shows the (provisioning AP) power-cap line.

  2. On the phone, join the open hotspot. Let the page open, or browse to the address. Choose your network, type its password, and submit. The phone drops off the hotspot when A1 reports success.

  3. If a code appears, enter it only on the service's own activation page.

  4. When A1 shows standby, leave the monitor with Ctrl+].

0:52Speaker on

  1. Unplug USB.

  2. Loosen both screws, slide one speaker lead into each terminal, and tighten until a gentle tug does not pull it out. Don't cut, strip or re-terminate the leads.

  3. Lay the speaker face-up on the desk, with its leads clear of each other and of every other wire.

0:55Ask it something

  1. Plug USB in. When A1 reconnects you may hear a short chime. That is the first sound, and it will be quiet.

  2. Put your ear close, quick-press the button, and ask a question. The reply should come out of the speaker as well as onto the OLED.

  3. Ask four more. Five replies in a row without a reset make PASS 11C.

Too quiet?

At cap 15 the speaker gets about 2 % of full amplitude, so quiet is expected. Cap 30 is the natural next step. By the same datasheet arithmetic it stays under 1 V at any gain setting, still 2.6 times below the speaker's limit. It is one line in firmware/sdkconfig.defaults and a rebuild. Leave that for the next session and finish this one at 15.
