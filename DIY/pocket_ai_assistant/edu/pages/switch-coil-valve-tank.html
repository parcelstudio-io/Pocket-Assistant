Switch, Coil, Valve, Tank

How a MOSFET, an inductor, a diode and a capacitor combine into a buck or boost converter — and why that beats a resistor every time.

The question this answers

You have 3.7 V from a battery and the amplifier wants 5 V. Or you have 5 V from USB and the chip wants 3.3 V. Neither is a job a resistor can do well. So what actually moves voltage from one value to another — and how do four ordinary components conspire to do it at 90% efficiency?

1. The bad way, so the good way makes sense

The obvious way to get 3.3 V from 5 V is to throw away the difference. That is a linear regulator: a valve that sits partly closed and burns off the excess pressure as heat.

It works, it is cheap, and it is quiet. But the arithmetic is brutal. Pass 1 A through it and it drops 1.7 V, so it dissipates 1.7 V × 1 A = 1.7 W as heat to deliver 3.3 W. That is 66% efficiency, and it does not improve — it is set by the voltage ratio alone.

Why it cannot be fixed

A linear regulator is like slowing a car by dragging your feet. The energy has to go somewhere, and in a linear regulator it always goes into heat. There is no clever version. The only escape is to stop throttling and start switching.

2. The four parts

A switching converter contains almost nothing. Four components, one job each.

The analogy, in one place

Think of a plumbing loop. Voltage is pressure and current is flow.

The MOSFET is a tap you can slam fully open or fully shut, thousands of times a second. The inductor is a long, heavy pipe full of moving water — water has mass, so once it is moving it does not want to stop. The diode is a check valve, letting flow pass one way and blocking it the other. The capacitor is a pressure tank that smooths pulses into a steady supply.

3. The one idea that makes it work

Everything below depends on a single property of the inductor, so it is worth stating on its own:

Current through an inductor cannot change instantly.

Voltage across it can jump anywhere it likes. The current cannot. It ramps. That is exactly the water in the heavy pipe: you can slam the tap shut in a millisecond, but the water already moving in the pipe keeps moving, because it has momentum.

4. Buck: stepping down by chopping time

To get a lower voltage, do not throttle the flow — open the tap fully, but only some of the time. The inductor and capacitor average the pulses back into a smooth, lower voltage.

The output is just the input multiplied by the fraction of time the switch is closed. That fraction is called the duty cycle, D.

V_out = D × V_in     5 V × 0.66 = 3.3 V
Leave the diode out and here is what happens

The moving current has nowhere to go. It forces the voltage at that node to climb — hundreds of volts within microseconds — until something breaks down and conducts. This is why an inductive load without a catch diode destroys the transistor driving it, and it is the same physics as the bang you hear from old plumbing when a valve shuts too fast: water hammer.

5. Boost: stepping up by harnessing the hammer

Now do something that looks impossible: get more voltage out than you put in. Same four parts, rearranged.

V_out = V_in / (1 − D)     3.7 V / (1 − 0.26) = 5 V

Nothing is created. The converter trades current for voltage: to deliver 1 A at 5 V from a 3.7 V battery, it must draw about 1.4 A from the cell. Power in still equals power out, minus losses.

6. Why this beats a resistor

Here is the whole efficiency argument in one line. Power is voltage times current. Look at the MOSFET in each of its two states:

Switch stateVoltage across itCurrent through itPower wasted
Fully ON~0 Vhigh0 × high ≈ 0
Fully OFFhigh0 Ahigh × 0 ≈ 0
Linear regulator1.7 V1 A1.7 W, always

One of the two terms is always nearly zero. A switch never sits in the middle where both are large — that is precisely what a linear regulator does for a living. Real converters lose a little during the switching transition and in the coil's resistance, which is why you see 85–95% rather than 100%.

On your devices

Cyber Dawn has one of each, in series, and that is the problem. The BQ25185 board boosts the 3.7 V cell to 5 V with a TPS61023 switching boost at roughly 90%. That 5 V then reaches the DevKit, whose onboard linear regulator drops it to 3.3 V at about 66%.

Multiply: 0.90 × 0.66 = 59%. The OLED needs 165 mW and costs about 280 mW at the battery. The fix in the production design is to delete the boost and use a switching buck straight to 3.3 V — which is exactly what docs/ARCHITECTURE.md recommends for the custom carrier.

Self-check

Q1. Why does a buck converter need the diode at all?

Because when the switch opens, the inductor's current cannot stop instantly and must have a path. The diode provides it. Without one, the voltage at that node spikes until something breaks down — the electrical version of water hammer.

Q2. A boost gives 5 V out from 3.7 V in. Where does the extra voltage come from?

From current. Energy is conserved: delivering 1 A at 5 V requires roughly 1.4 A drawn from the 3.7 V cell. Voltage went up, current went down, power stayed the same minus losses.

Q3. Why is a linear regulator's efficiency fixed by the voltage ratio alone?

It passes the same current it draws, and burns the voltage difference as heat. Efficiency is just V_out / V_in — 3.3 / 5 = 66%, no matter the load or the design.

Q4. What is the capacitor actually doing?

Holding the output steady between switching cycles. The inductor delivers current in a sawtooth; the capacitor absorbs the ripple so the load sees a smooth voltage. Remove it and your 3.3 V rail becomes a 3.3 V average with a visible sawtooth on top.
