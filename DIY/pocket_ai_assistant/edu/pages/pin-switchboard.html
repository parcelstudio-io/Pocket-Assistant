Before you start

Day 2 was about resistors — the tug-of-war rope, and why a pull has to be weak enough to lose. This page is about the thing on the other end of that rope: the pin itself. Everything here is reading and paper. Nothing on this page is an instruction to wire or touch anything.

Safety

The prototype is USB-only. The battery, charger, amplifier, speaker, and brass frame stay held until a reviewed power design exists, and the 3V3 rail has exactly one source: the board’s regulator.

1. What a pin actually is

Strip away the software and a GPIO pin is a small metal pad on the silicon, bonded by a hair-thin wire to a leg on the chip package, soldered to a pad on your board, with a number printed next to it. That is the whole of the physical story.

The interesting part is what sits behind that pad inside the chip. Four pieces of machinery, all of them permanently wired to the same pad, each one switched on or off by software:

  • An output driver that can shove the wire up to 3.3 V or down to 0 V.
  • An input buffer that watches the wire and reports back HIGH or LOW.
  • A pull-up resistor with a switch — your Day 2 child, pulling toward 3.3 V.
  • A pull-down resistor with a switch — the same child, pulling toward ground.
The idea to hold on to

A pin does not become an input or an output. Every pin is already all of these things at once, with the parts switched off. Setting a pin as an output just means “switch the output driver on.” That is why a pin can change its mind at runtime, and why forgetting to switch something off is a whole category of bug.

2. The switchboard

Here is the fact that surprises nearly everybody: on a modern chip, almost no pin has a fixed job. The roles in your config.h are choices somebody made, not facts about the silicon.

Inside the ESP32-C3 there are a handful of small specialised machines. Think of them as appliances: an I²C controller, an I²S controller, a PWM generator, timers, UARTs. Each one handles fast, precisely timed signalling that software running in a loop could never keep up with. Then there is a crossbar — Espressif calls it the GPIO matrix — that can patch almost any appliance’s signal onto almost any physical pin.

This is why every pin in your config file is a #define. Somebody typed GPIO_NUM_2 for the bit clock. They could have typed GPIO_NUM_7. The chip would not have cared.

A short history, because it explains the shape

Older chips did not work this way. The ATmega328 in a classic Arduino Uno had hard-wired pin roles: I²C lived on A4 and A5, and nowhere else, ever. If your board layout wanted them somewhere else, your layout lost. Modern chips — ESP32, STM32, RP2040 — added the crossbar, and pin assignment turned from a lookup into a design decision. That is why pinout arguments are a thing now and were not in 2010.

What the crossbar cannot do

It moves appliances around. It does not clone them. The ESP32-C3 has one I²C controller, one I²S, two SPI (one permanently busy running the flash chip your firmware lives in), and two UARTs. You may put I²C on any pin pair you like. You may not have three I²C buses.

There is also a speed footnote. The chip has two routing paths: IO_MUX is a short direct path, fastest, but each peripheral gets exactly one IO_MUX pin; the GPIO matrix is the flexible crossbar, reaching any pin at a cost of a couple of clock cycles. Everything on your board uses the matrix, happily. The distinction only starts to bite at very high clock rates.

Not every pin is equally free. Sort them into three tiers and the whole pinout stops looking arbitrary.

Look at the gaps, not the steady state

GPIO2 and GPIO5 are both driven push-pull once firmware is up. By a “what does it do normally” test, neither would need a resistor. They need one purely for the window before firmware exists. config.h says it outright for GPIO5: “An external pull-down keeps the amplifier in shutdown through reset.” Without it the pin floats for a few milliseconds and the MAX98357A can wake up unmuted — a pop, or a speaker at full gain, before any code runs to stop it.

4. Push-pull, open-drain, and why I²C needs pull-ups

The output driver from §1 comes in two flavours, and the difference decides whether a pull-up is optional or mandatory.

That trade is the entire design of I²C. On your OLED bus, no device is permitted to drive the line high. If two devices could push in opposite directions at once they would short through each other and cook. So everybody is limited to pulling down, the pull-up resistors do the lifting, and “talking” means yanking the line down against them. No pull-ups, no bus — the line never returns to HIGH and nothing works at all.

On your device

Your firmware turns the chip’s internal pull-ups on for the display bus — bus_config.flags.enable_internal_pullup = 1. That is the single most common job a pull-up ever does, and it is load-bearing rather than defensive: on a button, a missing pull-up gives you a flaky read; on I²C, a missing pull-up gives you nothing at all.

5. Your two buses

The names look almost identical and the jobs could not be more different.

I²C — a conversation with name-calling

Two wires, SDA for data and SCL for the clock, shared by every device on the bus. Before speaking, the controller says an address out loud. Every device hears it; only the one whose name was called answers. Your OLED answers to 0x3C — and your firmware tries 0x3D too, because Adafruit’s breakouts default to the other address. It runs at 400 kHz, which is slow by chip standards and entirely adequate: a display needs “put this picture up,” not a continuous torrent.

I²S — a marching band

Audio cannot tolerate “I’ll get to it in a moment.” Sixteen thousand samples every second, forever, each one on time. So I²S spends separate dedicated wires on timing: BCLK ticks once per bit, WS flips once per sample and its level says which channel, and the data lines carry the actual bits.

Notice the numbers agree: 16 000 samples per second × 64 bit-ticks per frame = 1 024 000 ticks per second. The 1.024 MHz in your config is not a magic number; it falls out of the other two.

The shape of the wiring

GPIO1 and GPIO2 each run to two destinations — the microphone and the amplifier both. The clocks are shared, because both devices must march to the same beat. The data lines are separate, because data flows in opposite directions: GPIO3 out to the amp, GPIO4 in from the mic. One conductor, two players.

6. Four layers of code

Your firmware claims pins in four different ways, from raw to convenient. Knowing which layer you are at tells you how much you are responsible for.

Layer 0
The #define in config.h — the single source of truth.
Layer 1
Raw gpio_* calls — you flip every switch yourself.
Layer 2
A pin number in a peripheral’s config struct — the driver programs the crossbar.
Layer 3
A helper object that hides all of it behind callbacks.

Layer 0 — one place, and only one place

#define AUDIO_I2S_GPIO_BCLK   GPIO_NUM_2
#define ACTION_BUTTON_GPIO    GPIO_NUM_10
#define DISPLAY_SDA_PIN       GPIO_NUM_21

Every layer below reads from here, and no bare pin number appears anywhere else. That discipline is what lets you re-pin the whole board by editing one file — and it is what makes it impossible to accidentally assign one pin to two owners.

Layer 1 — raw GPIO, one bit at a time

This is your amplifier mute. Read the order carefully, because it is load-bearing:

gpio_set_level(AMPLIFIER_ENABLE_GPIO, 0);    // 1. decide the value
gpio_pullup_dis(AMPLIFIER_ENABLE_GPIO);      // 2. nobody pulls up
gpio_pulldown_en(AMPLIFIER_ENABLE_GPIO);     // 3. internal pull-down holds LOW
gpio_set_direction(AMPLIFIER_ENABLE_GPIO, GPIO_MODE_OUTPUT);   // 4. NOW drive it

It sets the value before switching the driver on. Swap steps 1 and 4 and the pin would briefly drive whatever stale bit was sitting in the output latch — and on this pin, that is an unmuted amplifier. Notice too that step 2 exists at all: the internal pull-up and the pull-down would otherwise both be on, forming a divider and landing the pin in the undefined band.

Reading is the mirror image, and the == 0 is the active-low convention from Day 2:

int  level   = gpio_get_level(ACTION_BUTTON_GPIO);
bool pressed = (level == 0);   // pressed pulls the pin to ground

There is also a bundled form that sets every switch at once:

gpio_config_t io = {
    .pin_bit_mask = 1ULL << GPIO_NUM_6,   // which pins — a bitmask, so several at once
    .mode         = GPIO_MODE_INPUT,
    .pull_up_en   = GPIO_PULLUP_ENABLE,   // the Day 2 child, switched on
    .pull_down_en = GPIO_PULLDOWN_DISABLE,
    .intr_type    = GPIO_INTR_NEGEDGE,    // wake me on a falling edge
};
gpio_config(&io);

Layer 2 — hand the pin to an appliance

This is where the crossbar actually gets programmed, and it looks like nothing at all:

bus_config.sda_io_num = DISPLAY_SDA_PIN;          // GPIO21
bus_config.scl_io_num = DISPLAY_SCL_PIN;          // GPIO20
bus_config.flags.enable_internal_pullup = 1;
i2c_new_master_bus(&bus_config, &display_i2c_bus_);

Those two assignments are the dots in the crossbar diagram. You never call a “route this signal” function. You put a pin number in a struct field, the driver programs the matrix, and from that moment the I²C hardware owns GPIO20 and GPIO21. Change 21 to 7, rebuild, and the bus comes out of GPIO7 instead.

Audio works identically — four pins, four arguments:

NoAudioCodecDuplex(AUDIO_INPUT_SAMPLE_RATE, AUDIO_OUTPUT_SAMPLE_RATE,
                   AUDIO_I2S_GPIO_BCLK, AUDIO_I2S_GPIO_WS,
                   AUDIO_I2S_GPIO_DOUT, AUDIO_I2S_GPIO_DIN)

Layer 3 — a helper object

PocketWallEC3Board() : action_button_(ACTION_BUTTON_GPIO) { ... }

action_button_.OnClick([this]() { Application::GetInstance().ToggleChatState(); });
action_button_.OnLongPress([this]() { EnterWifiConfigMode(); });

The Button class takes the pin number, enables the pull-up, debounces the contact chatter, times long presses, and calls your function. You hand it a pin and get events back. Everything from §1 still happens — somebody else just wrote it.

7. Claiming a spare pin

Three worked examples, in increasing order of cleverness.

A status LED, with brightness

#define STATUS_LED_GPIO GPIO_NUM_6      // Layer 0: declare it once

ledc_timer_config_t t = {
    .speed_mode = LEDC_LOW_SPEED_MODE, .duty_resolution = LEDC_TIMER_10_BIT,
    .timer_num  = LEDC_TIMER_0, .freq_hz = 5000, .clk_cfg = LEDC_AUTO_CLK,
};
ledc_timer_config(&t);

ledc_channel_config_t c = {
    .gpio_num   = STATUS_LED_GPIO,      // <-- the crossbar again, one field
    .speed_mode = LEDC_LOW_SPEED_MODE, .channel = LEDC_CHANNEL_0,
    .timer_sel  = LEDC_TIMER_0, .duty = 0, .hpoint = 0,
};
ledc_channel_config(&c);

ledc_set_duty(LEDC_LOW_SPEED_MODE, LEDC_CHANNEL_0, 512);   // half brightness
ledc_update_duty(LEDC_LOW_SPEED_MODE, LEDC_CHANNEL_0);

Plus the series resistor from Day 2 — 270 Ω for a red LED at about 5 mA. The chip will happily push enough current to destroy the LED if you let it.

A second button

Button mute_button_{GPIO_NUM_7};
mute_button_.OnClick([this]() { ToggleMute(); });

Two lines, because Layer 3 already exists. Pick a non-strap, non-USB pin and match the active-low convention so firmware treats both buttons the same way.

The trick worth memorising

You have one I²C bus with one device on it. Want to add a temperature sensor, a real-time clock and an accelerometer? Zero new pins. They all hang on the same GPIO20 and GPIO21, each answering to its own address; you add one i2c_master_bus_add_device() call per chip and nothing else. Before you spend a pin, always ask whether the part comes in an I²C version. Pin scarcity is usually a design choice, not a wall.

8. Changing a pin, and five ways to get burned

1
Edit firmware/src/boards/pocket-wall-e-c3/config.h.
2
Rebuild: ./firmware/scripts/build.sh
3
Reflash: ./firmware/scripts/flash.sh
4
Move the wire.

Step 4 is the one people forget. Software cannot move a jumper. That #define is a description of how your board is physically wired, not a control over it. Change the number without moving the wire and you have simply told the chip to talk to an empty pin, which fails silently and looks like a broken peripheral.

Five ways to get burned

  1. gpio_reset_pin() is not neutral. Your own code carries a warning about this: it restores a pin to its factory default, and for GPIO5 that default includes an internal pull-up — which would un-mute your amplifier. “Reset to default” sounds safe. It is not. Defaults are opinions somebody else had.
  2. Don’t reassign a strap casually. GPIO2, GPIO8, GPIO9. Fine for something undriven at boot, a trap otherwise. The vendor image put microphone data on GPIO8; if the mic drove it LOW at reset you could lose download mode, and with no OTA slots that is unrecoverable inside a sealed frame.
  3. Not every pin can do everything. Analog input only works on ADC-capable pads. The crossbar routes digital signals; an analog voltage cannot travel through it.
  4. Respect the current limit. A GPIO sources tens of milliamps at most. It drives a transistor, a logic input, a small LED. It does not drive a motor, a relay coil, or a speaker.
  5. Two owners, one pin. Assign GPIO7 to both a button and a peripheral and whichever initialises last silently wins. No error, no warning. Keeping every pin number in config.h is what makes this impossible.

Self-check

Q1. Your firmware sets a pin as an input. What happened to the output driver?

Nothing — it is still physically there, just switched off. Every pin has all four pieces of machinery at all times; configuring a pin only decides which ones are enabled and what the MUX is listening to.

Q2. Could the I²S bit clock have been put on GPIO7 instead of GPIO2?

Yes. BCLK is a Tier 3 assignment — change the #define, rebuild, move the wire. You would also delete the 10 kΩ pull-up, because that resistor exists only to satisfy the strap requirement on GPIO2, and GPIO7 is not a strap.

Q3. Why can a push-pull output skip the pull resistor when an open-drain output cannot?

Push-pull actively drives both directions, so the line is never undriven — there is no gap to cover. Open-drain can only pull down; without a pull-up nothing on the wire is capable of producing a HIGH at all.

Q4. You add three I²C sensors to your board. How many GPIOs does that cost?

Zero. They share SDA and SCL with the OLED and are told apart by address. The cost is bus bandwidth and one add_device call each.

Q5. You change AUDIO_I2S_GPIO_DIN from GPIO4 to GPIO6, rebuild and reflash. The microphone goes silent. Why?

Almost certainly because the wire is still on GPIO4. Software cannot move a jumper. Second suspect: the 100 kΩ pull-down is also still on GPIO4, so the new pin has nothing resting it at silence.

Q6. Why does GPIO5 need an external pull-down when firmware drives it push-pull anyway?

Because of the gap before firmware exists. For the first milliseconds after reset nothing drives the pin, and the amplifier is reading it. The resistor guarantees shutdown through that window.
