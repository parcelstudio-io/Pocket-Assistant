Why the brass build works

Ten short chapters on the physics and engineering behind the rules in the Brass Wiring page: why two rails, why a resistor here and a capacitor there, why one corner stays empty, why two irons' worth of temperature. Written for a software engineer; each chapter has one picture and one analogy. About forty minutes, no bench needed.

Chapter 1 of 10Everything is a loop

Analogy: every call needs a return. A wire with no ground path is a function that never returns: nothing happens, and nothing tells you why.
In the brass build, this is why
  • one GND rail that every module taps
  • the thick ground rod for the amp: the biggest return current gets its own path
  • the meter check after each rod: a rail touching the frame makes the frame part of a loop

Chapter 2 of 10Volts, amps, ohms

Analogy: voltage is pressure, current is flow, resistance is pipe width. The rods are wide pipes; the resistors are deliberate pinholes.
In the brass build, this is why
  • bare rod is an excellent conductor; the spacing rules are the whole cost
  • resistor values are chosen for how much current they leak (V/R), not for the signal

Chapter 3 of 10Two voltages, one source

Analogy: 3.3 V and 5 V are two incompatible API versions. The regulator is the adapter; the amp speaks the old one natively.
In the brass build, this is why
  • the 3V3 rail feeds the screen and mic; the single 5 V rod feeds the amp
  • the frame isn't powered, so it belongs to neither rail
  • one USB source means there is never a moment when one part is on and another off

Chapter 4 of 10Pulls and defaults

Analogy: a pull resistor is a default argument. The caller (the chip driver) can pass a value; if it doesn't, the default applies.
In the brass build, this is why
  • 10 kΩ on GPIO2 (boot pin) to 3V3 rail
  • 100 kΩ on the mic's data line to GND rail
  • the amp's SD pull-down to GND rail: the amp stays off through reset
  • button: A1's internal pull-up, so pressed = pulled to ground

Chapter 5 of 10Capacitors: the local cache

Analogy: the 220 µF is an L1 cache next to the CPU; the USB cable is main memory. Loud syllables are cache hits or they are stalls.
In the brass build, this is why
  • 220 µF + 100 nF soldered with the shortest possible leads at the amp's VIN and GND
  • why the meter chirps, then stops, between VIN and GND

Chapter 6 of 10I²C: the slow bus

Analogy: a shared bus with device addresses, master-initiated, acknowledged per byte. Think a tiny, polite RPC over two wires.
In the brass build, this is why
  • two rods, SCL and SDA, to the screen, routed wherever is tidy
  • the pull-ups live on the screen module, so nothing extra is needed

Chapter 7 of 10I²S: the fast bus, and frequency

Analogy: a fixed-rate stream with a frame clock and a bit clock, like a PCM audio buffer on the wire.
In the brass build, this is why
  • WS and BCLK rods each have two taps: mic and amp share the clocks
  • the mic's L/R link to GND picks the left slot
  • these are the rods to keep away from the antenna corner

Chapter 8 of 10The amplifier and the speaker

Analogy: the bridge output is a differential signal; the speaker reads the difference, like a differential pair, so there is no ground reference to borrow.
In the brass build, this is why
  • OUT+ and OUT− never touch GND, a rail or the frame
  • the 100 kΩ GAIN resistor to the GND rail = 15 dB
  • the volume cap stays at 45 unless the scope measures a higher one

Chapter 9 of 10The antenna and the near field

Analogy: nearby metal is a side effect you didn't declare: it changes the antenna's behaviour without any explicit connection.
In the brass build, this is why
  • the back-left corner stays empty with nothing above it
  • the frame is never ground
  • the fast I²S rods stay on the other side of the box

Chapter 10 of 10Heat, solder and flux

Analogy: two environments with separate tooling. Brass tip and acid flux are prod-only ops tools; they never run against the dev boards.
In the brass build, this is why
  • brass joints: 370–400 °C, acid flux, then wash
  • module joints: 330–350 °C, Chip Quik flux, 1–2 s, electronics tip
  • wash the frame before any part comes near it

Check yourself

Why does the meter chirp between VIN and GND, then go quiet?

The empty 220 µF capacitor takes a gulp of current and looks like a short for a moment; once charged it stops. A steady tone would be a real short.

Why can't I just ground the brass frame like the video?

Both speaker outputs are live, the 5 V rod is live, and a grounded cage next to the antenna acts as a ground plane. One accidental touch becomes a short, and Wi-Fi gets worse.

Why is a 1 mm brass rod fine for a 1 MHz clock?

At 1 MHz the wavelength is 300 m; a 40 mm rod is electrically a dot. It only matters that it stays clear of other rods and the antenna.

Why 10 kΩ and not 100 Ω for the pull-up?

The chip's driver must be able to override it, and a 100 Ω pull-up would waste 33 mA whenever the line is low. 10 kΩ wastes 0.33 mA and still wins against noise.

Why does the frame get a hotter iron than the chips?

Brass is a big heat sink; the joint must reach 183 °C fast. A chip pin can take about 260 °C for ten seconds, so module joints are quick and cooler.
