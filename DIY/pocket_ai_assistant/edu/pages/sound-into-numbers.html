Sound is pressure 0:00–0:10

Clap your hands. Your palms shove the air, that air shoves the air beside it, and the shove travels outward at about 343 metres per second. Nothing moves from hand to ear except pressure rising above and dipping below the room's resting pressure. That pattern is sound.

Frequency, in hertz (Hz), is cycles per second and sets the pitch; speech lives mostly between 100 Hz and 4 kHz. Amplitude, the pressure swing from rest, sets loudness. The period T is the time for one cycle.

f = 1 / T → hertz = 1 / seconds

Ears span an enormous range: a whisper and a rock concert differ in power by about a hundred million times, so sound uses decibels (dB), a log scale. Every 10 dB is ten times the power; every 6 dB doubles pressure, or voltage.

dB = 10 × log10(P2/P1) for power; 20 × log10(A2/A1) for pressure or voltage
Software analogy

Decibels are the log axis on a latency chart: ratios, not differences.

Microphones: turning pressure into a moving plate 0:10–0:20

Every microphone starts with a diaphragm, a thin membrane the pressure wave pushes back and forth. A MEMS microphone etches it into silicon a hair's breadth from a fixed backplate, forming a capacitor, Day 3's two plates with a gap. Sound moves the diaphragm, the capacitance changes, and with a fixed charge the voltage between the plates wobbles with the sound.

That wobble is millivolts, easy prey on a wire for noise from the Wi-Fi radio, the OLED, and the USB supply. An analog electret microphone sends that fragile voltage to an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) in the microcontroller. A digital I2S microphone such as your INMP441 converts inside the can, so the wire carries numbers, and noise on a logic level is ignored.

On your device

The AITRIP INMP441 is a MEMS microphone with its ADC built in, powered only from the SuperMini's 3.3 V pin, data on GPIO4. It beat an electret plus the ESP32-C3's own ADC, which is noisy, shares a die with a radio, and would need a preamplifier.

Sampling: looking at the wave often enough 0:20–0:35

The ADC takes a snapshot N times per second; N is the sample rate. The Nyquist rule: to capture a frequency f, sample at more than 2f. Sample slower and a fast wiggle masquerades as a slow one, called aliasing, and nothing can undo it afterwards.

Your firmware samples at 16,000 Hz, capturing anything below 8 kHz: speech with room to spare, and Opus's native rate.

Bit depth is how finely each snapshot is rounded: 24-bit signed integers from the INMP441, each in a 32-bit slot whose extra 8 bits are padding, discarded with raw >> 8.

Once a second the diagnostics print a MIC24 line with min, max, and rms. Root mean square is the fair "average size" of a signal that swings both ways: square every sample, average, take the root. Loudness and heating both follow rms.

rms = √( (x1² + x2² + … + xN²) / N ) → counts

The I2S bus: three wires, one clock 0:35–0:50

Yesterday's I2C bus was a conversation: address, request, acknowledge. Audio is a firehose of 16,000 numbers a second, so I2S (Inter-IC Sound) streams with no addresses and no acknowledgements.

  • BCLK, the bit clock, ticks once per bit. Yours: GPIO2.
  • WS, word select, completes one cycle per frame: low for the left slot, high for the right. Yours: GPIO1, at the sample rate, 16 kHz.
  • Data. The mic's SD drives numbers in on GPIO4; the finished device drives numbers out to the amplifier's DIN from GPIO3.
BCLK = 16,000 frames/s × 2 slots × 32 bits = 1,024,000 bits/s = 1.024 MHz

Why share BCLK and WS? The ESP32-C3 has one I2S peripheral, run in duplex mode: one clock generator, data in on one pin, out on another. It saves two GPIOs but forces one sample rate on both directions.

On your device

Two breadboard resistors belong here: a 10 kΩ pull-up on GPIO2, a boot strap pin the ROM reads at reset that must not float, and a 100 kΩ pull-down on GPIO4, parking the data line. The vendor image used GPIO8, also a strap pin and the onboard LED, so the source port moved it. Never mix the two pinouts.

Why 16 kHz and not the vendor's 24 kHz 0:50–0:55

The vendor image runs the bus at 24 kHz; the source port runs it at 16 kHz, because of one datasheet line: the MAX98357A supports 8, 16, 32, 44.1, 48, 88.2, and 96 kHz, and 24 kHz is listed as not supported. The amplifier shares WS with the microphone, so the microphone must use the amplifier's rate; 16 kHz is legal for both and native for Opus. Nobody ran a test; someone read the table and changed a constant in config.h.

Speakers and amplifiers: theory only this week 0:55–1:10

Safety

The prototype is USB-only. The MAX98357A, speaker, battery, charger, and brass frame stay off it until a reviewed power design exists; the firmware holds the amplifier enable, GPIO5, low. Nothing here is an instruction to wire anything, and no test of the amplifier or speaker exists yet.

A speaker is a microphone run backwards: a coil in a magnet's field, glued to a cone. Current shoves the coil one way, reversed current the other, and the cone pumps the air. Your Same Sky speaker is rated 8 Ω, its impedance (the AC cousin of resistance), and 0.8 W, the heat its coil can survive; together they fix the largest rms voltage it may see.

P = V² / R → V = √(P × R) = √(0.8 W × 8 Ω) ≈ 2.53 V rms

A GPIO pin cannot drive that: 2.5 V into 8 Ω is over 300 mA, and a pin manages a few tens of milliamps at 0 or 3.3 V, never a smooth wave. So the chip sends numbers to an amplifier, which shapes supply energy to follow them.

A classic amplifier is a variable resistor between supply and speaker, wasting as heat whatever it does not deliver. A class-D amplifier's transistors are instead fully on (no voltage drop, so no heat) or fully off (no current, so no heat), switching at hundreds of kilohertz, with the on-fraction following the audio. The coil's inductance smooths the pulses, so the cone follows the audio underneath.

The MAX98357A adds BTL, bridge-tied load: it drives both speaker wires in opposite directions, so the speaker sees the difference, twice the swing from one supply. The cost is a rule with no exceptions: neither speaker wire is ground. Ground either, or touch it to the brass frame, and you short half the bridge.

On your device

The HiLetgo MAX98357A takes I2S directly, needs no heatsink, and gives usable loudness from 3.3 V. GPIO5 is its enable, held low by the firmware, so even a wired amplifier stays silent until someone deliberately changes that.

Sensitivity, rms numbers, and the missing pass mark 1:10–1:15

The INMP441 datasheet gives a typical sensitivity of −26 dBFS: a 1 kHz tone at 94 dB SPL lands 26 dB below full scale, about one-twentieth of the range. Conversation at 30 cm is roughly 30 dB quieter, so its rms lands in the low tens of thousands. Datasheet expectations, not values from your board.

The quickstart gives no single pass number because rms depends on distance, room, voice, and breadboard hum. A healthy microphone gives a repeatable change: quiet, speech, quiet, and rms rises and falls back every time. Judge the pattern, not the number.

Worked examples 1:15–1:45

Try each on paper before reading the steps; about five minutes each.

Example 1. Your I2S wire budget

Scenario. The INMP441 streams at 16 kHz in two 32-bit slots, and the assistant sends compressed speech over Wi-Fi. How much of the traffic on the wire actually leaves the device?

Step 1, the wire rate. Every bit slot on the I2S wire, used or not.

16,000 frames/s × 2 slots × 32 bits = 1,024,000 bit/s

Step 2, the useful bits. Only the left slot carries data, and only 24 of its 32 bits count.

16,000 × 24 = 384,000 bit/s

Step 3, what the encoder takes. Opus works on 16-bit samples.

16,000 × 16 = 256,000 bit/s

Step 4, what the radio sends. Opus speech is about 24 kbit/s.

1,024,000 ÷ 24,000 ≈ 43 → 1 bit sent for every 43 on GPIO4

For a designer: the microphone's wire is busy, but the radio sends very little. On a battery device, the radio, not the microphone, sets battery life.

Example 2. The speaker's ceiling

Scenario. A bridge amplifier fed from V volts can ideally swing a peak of V across the coil. Could a 3.3 V supply overdrive your 8 Ω, 0.8 W speaker? Could 5 V?

Step 1, the limit. From the equation in the speakers section.

V = √(0.8 W × 8 Ω) ≈ 2.53 V rms

Step 2, from 3.3 V. The largest clean sine has a 3.3 V peak.

3.3 ÷ √2 ≈ 2.33 V rms → 2.33² ÷ 8 ≈ 0.68 W, about 0.29 A

Under the limit.

Step 3, from 5 V.

5 ÷ √2 ≈ 3.54 V rms → 3.54² ÷ 8 ≈ 1.56 W

Nearly double the rating.

For a designer: at 3.3 V the supply itself protects the speaker; at 5 V only the firmware's volume cap does. Your amplifier now runs from the 5 V pin, which is why A1's firmware caps the volume.

Example 3. A phone camera and a ceiling fan

Scenario. A phone films a three-blade ceiling fan turning at 580 rpm, at 30 frames per second. What does the video show?

Step 1, how often a blade passes.

3 blades × 580 rpm ÷ 60 s = 29 per second

Step 2, the camera's limit. Nyquist at 30 frames per second is 15 Hz, and 29 is above it, so aliasing is certain.

Step 3, what you see. The alias is the distance to the nearest multiple of the frame rate.

|29 − 30| = 1 Hz → the fan seems to creep backwards, one blade-step per second

Step 4, the fixes. Sample faster, for example 240 fps, or blur the blades first with a longer shutter.

For a designer: the INMP441 uses the second fix. A built-in filter removes everything above 8 kHz before its converter samples, so ultrasonic whine cannot fold down into the speech band.

Predict, then reveal

Thought experiment 1. A contributor changes one constant in config.h from 16,000 to 24,000 "to match the vendor image", on a finished device whose amplifier shares WS and BCLK with the microphone. Predict: what does BCLK become, and does each end still work?

Reveal

BCLK becomes 24,000 × 64 = 1.536 MHz. The INMP441 keeps streaming, so MIC24 looks healthy; the MAX98357A does not support 24 kHz, so it stays muted or distorts. The fault hides on the side that exists.

Thought experiment 2. The INMP441's L/R pin is tied to 3.3 V instead of GND; everything else, including the 100 kΩ pull-down on GPIO4, is correct. What does SD carry in each half of the frame, and what does the firmware report?

Reveal

The microphone now answers the right slot: it drives its 24 bits while WS is high and releases the line while WS is low. The firmware reads the left slot and finds the line parked by the pull-down, so every sample is the same number: rms 0, min equal to max, a "no varying samples" warning. Nothing is broken; the two ends disagree about which slot is the conversation.

Self-check quiz 1:45–1:50

Q1. At 16 kHz, what is the highest frequency you can capture without aliasing, and does it cover speech?

8 kHz, half the sample rate. Speech carries nearly all its energy below that.

Q2. Why is the bit clock 1.024 MHz and not 16,000 × 24 = 384 kHz?

Each sample rides in a 32-bit slot and every frame has two slots, used or not.

Q3. Why does the INMP441's L/R pin go to GND, and could an I2C scan find the microphone?

L/R picks the mic's slot; GND means left, which the firmware reads. A scan finds nothing: I2S has no addresses.

Q4. Why did the source port move from the vendor's 24 kHz to 16 kHz?

The MAX98357A does not support 24 kHz, and it shares WS with the microphone.

Q5. Someone suggests grounding one speaker wire to the brass frame "for a cleaner signal". What is wrong?

The MAX98357A is BTL: both wires are driven and neither is ground, so grounding one shorts half the bridge. And this week the amplifier is not on the prototype.

Design-your-own-device: the baby monitor 1:50–2:00

Your device is a baby monitor: detect crying (300 Hz to 4 kHz) and play a lullaby through a 4 Ω, 1 W speaker. On paper, choose the sample rate, analog or I2S microphone, whether to share I2S clocks, the maximum amplifier output, and one datasheet line to check first.

Worked answer

Sample rate. Crying tops out at 4 kHz, so Nyquist demands more than 8 kHz; pick 16 kHz for margin, on the MAX98357A's list and native to Opus.

Microphone. I2S digital: near a Wi-Fi radio and maybe a charger, an electret's millivolts would collect noise on the way to a mediocre ADC.

Share clocks? Yes: both run at 16 kHz, and it saves two pins.

Maximum output. V = √(1 W × 4 Ω) = 2.0 V rms, about 2.83 V peak; more overheats the coil.

Datasheet line. Minimum load impedance and output power into 4 Ω at your supply; an 8 Ω-only chip will overheat or current-limit.
