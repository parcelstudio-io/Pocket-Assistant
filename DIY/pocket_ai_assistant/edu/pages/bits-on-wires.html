Logic levels 0:00–0:10

A wall switch is up or down, but watch someone flip it slowly and there is a moment when it is neither. Digital electronics has the same secret. The chip never sees a 1 or a 0. It sees a voltage on a wire and compares it with two thresholds.

On the ESP32-C3, anything above roughly 2.5 V (0.75 × VDD, where VDD is the 3.3 V supply) reads HIGH, a 1. Anything below roughly 0.8 V (0.25 × VDD) reads LOW, a 0. Between them is the forbidden middle. A pin at 1.5 V is not half a one; the datasheet makes no promise, so it may read 1, or 0, or flip between them as noise wiggles.

HIGH ≥ 0.75 × VDD ≈ 2.5 V · LOW ≤ 0.25 × VDD ≈ 0.8 V (VDD = 3.3 V)

The absolute maximum on any pin is only about 3.6 V. A 5 V signal is not "extra HIGH"; it forces current through the pin's protection structure and can kill the pin or the chip. That is why it matters that the OLED and the INMP441 are 3.3 V parts: powered at 3.3 V, talking at 3.3 V, never exceeding what the ESP32-C3 accepts.

On your device

The OLED and the INMP441 are powered from the SuperMini's 3.3 V pin and nothing else, so every signal on the board, SDA, SCL, WS, BCLK, mic data and the button, is a 3.3 V signal. The 5 V USB rail never touches a data pin.

What a GPIO pin really is 0:10–0:22

GPIO means general-purpose input/output. Behind each pin sit three tiny circuits, and software picks which one is active.

First, an output driver: two switches in a column, one to 3.3 V and one to ground (GND), with exactly one closed at a time. This is push-pull. Writing a 1 closes the upper switch; a 0 closes the lower one.

Second, an input comparator: the threshold judge from the last section, reporting 1 or 0 into a register your code reads.

Third, optional pull resistors: a weak resistor, roughly 45 kΩ inside the ESP32-C3, switchable between the pin and 3.3 V (pull-up) or the pin and ground (pull-down). It gives the pin a default level so it never floats in the forbidden middle.

The switches are small transistors, so they are weak: a pin can source or sink only tens of milliamps, about 40 mA at most (the datasheet's maximum output current at the strongest drive setting). An LED through a resistor is fine; a speaker or motor straight from a pin is not. That is why the MAX98357A amplifier exists in your parts box (off the prototype this week): the pin sends a tiny data signal, and the amp supplies the speaker's current.

Software analogy

A pin is a one-bit register with a mode. Input mode polls the comparator; output mode moves the switches. Enabling a pull-up is setting a default value: if nobody assigns the variable, it reads 1.

On your device

GPIO10, the action button, is an input with the internal pull-up enabled. The button connects it to GND, so pressed reads 0 and released reads 1: "active-low". The wire is short, so 45 kΩ is enough and no external resistor was needed. GPIO20 and GPIO21 use a fourth mode, open-drain, the story of I2C below.

Reset, strap pins, and the BOOT button 0:22–0:35

On Day 2 you met the undefined window: the rail still rising, reset held, nothing trustworthy yet. Today's question is what happens the instant reset is released.

The chip wakes running code baked into its ROM, which reads three strap pins, GPIO2, GPIO8 and GPIO9, for at least 3 ms. Their levels answer one question: boot the app from flash, or wait for a computer to send a new image over USB? GPIO9 HIGH means normal boot; GPIO9 LOW with GPIO8 HIGH means download mode. GPIO2 must be HIGH for both answers: the datasheet lists no boot mode with GPIO2 LOW, so a LOW GPIO2 at reset is not a valid boot mode at all, and the board does not come up.

The SuperMini's BOOT button simply pulls GPIO9 to ground: held through a reset, it makes the ROM enter download mode for the flasher. So GPIO9 is not an application input. Anything holding it LOW during a reset makes the board boot into download mode and look dead, which is why the action button lives on GPIO10, a pin the ROM never reads.

The vendor image put mic data on GPIO8. On a SuperMini that is a strap pin that also drives the onboard blue LED, and a microphone's output could hold it LOW during the 3 ms window, refusing download mode. Inside a sealed frame with no way to disconnect the mic, a bad flash could never be fixed. The source port moved mic data to GPIO4, a pin with no boot meaning, with a 100 kΩ pull-down for a defined default before I2S takes over.

On your device

GPIO2 carries the I2S bit clock (BCLK) and has a 10 kΩ pull-up to 3V3 so it reads HIGH at boot. GPIO8 is left to the onboard LED. GPIO9 is BOOT and nothing else. Mic data is on GPIO4 with a 100 kΩ pull-down to GND. The vendor image's GPIO8 assignment and the source port's GPIO4 assignment are two different pinouts, and they never mix.

Software analogy

Strap pins are command-line flags read once at process start, before main() runs; nothing you write later changes what they meant. A live data line on a flag pin lets a random value pick recovery mode.

Your board, pin by pin

Here is A1 as it sits on your breadboard, with every pin labelled by what it connects to and the voltage it should rest at while the assistant sits idle, listening for its wake word. Most pins rest HIGH because something pulls them up: a strap pull-up, a button's pull-up, or the OLED module's bus pull-ups. The three audio pins never rest; they carry the I2S clocks and data all the time the microphone is listening.

Buttons and bouncing 0:35–0:43

A tactile button is two springy contacts. Pressed, they do not meet once; they bang together, separate and meet again for a few milliseconds. To you that is one press. To a comparator sampling thousands of times a second it is a burst of 0-1-0-1 before the line settles LOW.

Firmware fixes this by debouncing: after the first change, ignore the pin for a settle time (say 20 ms) and then read it once, or accept a new state only after N identical reads in a row. The window is longer than any bounce and shorter than the fastest deliberate double-press (about 100 ms), so nothing real is lost.

Software analogy

Debouncing is a retry with a settle timeout. A flapping health check does not mark a service down on the first failed check; it waits for the state to be stable for a window before acting.

Serial buses: UART, SPI, I2C 0:43–0:52

The OLED needs 1024 bytes of picture. Eight wires at once, a parallel bus, would cost eight pins plus a "now" line. Your SuperMini exposes only 13 usable GPIOs, several already spent, so the picture goes serially: one bit after another, in time, on one wire.

That raises one problem: when should the receiver look? A slow wire may still be rising when the sender moves on. The clean answer is a second wire, a clock, whose edges say "look now". The three buses you will meet differ in how they answer that and in how many devices share the wires.

I2C won for the OLED because it costs two pins however many devices you add later, and a text screen changes slowly, so SPI's speed is not needed. GPIO20 and GPIO21 would otherwise sit idle as the old UART0 lines that native USB replaced.

Software analogy

I2C addresses are ports on one network interface. SDA and SCL are the cable; the 7-bit address says which service should answer. UART is a point-to-point socket with no addressing; SPI gives each peer its own physical select line instead.

I2C in detail 0:52–1:07

Here is the idea that makes I2C shareable. On SDA and SCL no device may ever push the wire up to 3.3 V. Each device has only the lower switch of the push-pull pair: pull the line to ground, or let go. This is open-drain. One pull-up resistor per wire does the lifting: when everyone lets go, the resistor pulls the line to 3.3 V, the idle state.

Two push-pull outputs on one wire would fight, a short circuit between one shoving up and one shoving down. Open-drain devices cannot fight: if any device pulls LOW, the line is LOW. This "wired-AND" lets the OLED answer on the same wire the ESP32-C3 talks on.

The resistor's value is a compromise. Smaller rises faster after release, which 400 kHz needs, but every device must sink more current when LOW. Larger means less current but a lazier rise. Typical module values are 4.7 to 10 kΩ. Your Hosyond OLED carries its own pair, which is exactly why none were added to your breadboard: extra resistors would sit in parallel and change the value blindly.

I_sink = V / R = 3.3 V / 4.7 kΩ ≈ 0.7 mA per line while LOW

One transaction, bit by bit

The ESP32-C3 is the controller; the OLED is a target. Rule one: SDA changes only while SCL is LOW, and is read while SCL is HIGH. The two deliberate exceptions are the bookends: START is SDA falling while SCL stays HIGH; STOP is SDA rising while SCL stays HIGH.

Read it left to right. After START the controller sends seven address bits, most significant first: 0x3C is 0111100. The eighth bit is 0 for write, 1 for read. On the ninth clock the controller lets go of SDA. If a device with that address is alive it pulls SDA LOW: ACK. If nothing answers, the pull-up keeps SDA HIGH: NACK, nobody home. Each data byte follows the same pattern, eight bits then an ACK slot, and STOP releases the bus.

The OLED's address is set by a solder jumper on the module: generic modules ship at 0x3C, Adafruit's at 0x3D. The firmware tries 0x3C, then 0x3D, and if both NACK it boots headless instead of crashing, so a display fault never takes the rest of the device down. Whatever the silkscreen says, the address that ACKs is the truth.

On your device

SDA is GPIO21, SCL is GPIO20, 400 kHz, addresses 0x3C then 0x3D tried at boot. The external pull-ups are the Hosyond module's own, so the breadboard has none; the firmware also enables the C3's weak ~45 kΩ internal pull-up on both lines, which sits in parallel and changes the total only slightly. A second I2C module with its own pull-ups would land in parallel and drop the effective resistance; work out the sum first.

Software analogy

An I2C write is a request with a mandatory per-byte acknowledgement. A NACK on the address byte is "connection refused": the port exists on the cable, but nothing is listening.

The SSD1306 and its 1024-byte picture 1:07–1:15

The SSD1306 is the controller chip on the OLED module. It holds a framebuffer: one bit per pixel, 128 × 64 = 8192 bits = 1024 bytes. Your firmware keeps its own copy in RAM, draws text into it, and pushes the whole thing across I2C as one block.

1024 bytes × 9 clocks per byte = 9216 clocks → 9216 / 400 000 Hz ≈ 23 ms per frame

Each byte costs nine clocks, eight bits plus ACK, so a frame takes about 23 ms plus a little overhead. That caps refresh near 40 frames per second: slow for a game, plenty for a line of text, and it costs two pins.

Why an OLED rather than an LCD? Each pixel is its own light source. No backlight, so a mostly black screen draws very little current, exactly the white-on-black look this device uses. It runs from 3.3 V directly, so no extra regulator and no level shifting.

On your device

Hosyond 0.96" SSD1306, 128 × 64, I2C, powered from the SuperMini 3.3 V pin. DISPLAY_INVERT_COLOR is true so lit pixels appear as bright text on black, keeping current low. Identical-looking modules ship in two pin orders, GND-VCC-SCL-SDA or VCC-GND-SCL-SDA, and swapped power pins can destroy a module, which is why the silkscreen, not the look of the board, defines the wiring.

Safety

The prototype is USB-only. Battery, charger, amplifier, speaker and brass frame are held until a reviewed power design exists. Nothing on this page is an instruction to wire, change or test anything: this week is theory only, with no hands-on work on the prototype.

Worked examples 1:15–1:45

Paper only this week, so these examples put numbers on today's ideas. Cover the steps, try each one on paper, then compare. About five minutes each.

Example 1. Sharing the OLED's two wires

Scenario. Your Hosyond module carries a pull-up on SDA (GPIO21) and one on SCL (GPIO20); suppose each is 4.7 kΩ, a common value. You add a sensor board that carries its own 10 kΩ pair. The I2C standard asks every device to sink 3 mA and still hold its line below 0.4 V.

Step 1, one module. While the OLED holds SDA LOW for an ACK, the whole pull-up current flows into its switch.

I = V / R = 3.3 V / 4.7 kΩ ≈ 0.70 mA

Step 2, two modules. Two pull-ups from the same 3.3 V to the same wire are in parallel, so the pair acts like one smaller resistor.

R = (4.7 × 10) / (4.7 + 10) kΩ ≈ 3.2 kΩ → I ≈ 3.3 V / 3.2 kΩ ≈ 1.0 mA

Step 3, the floor. The budget is gone when the combined pull-up reaches 3.3 V / 3 mA = 1.1 kΩ. Four boards at 4.7 kΩ each give 1.2 kΩ, right at the edge.

For a designer: the 112 usable addresses are rarely the limit on an I2C bus; the pull-up budget is. The habit is one pair per bus, and cut the jumpers on every board you add.

Example 2. The screen's time budget at two bus speeds

Scenario. Suppose the firmware sends the whole framebuffer in one transaction: an address byte, a control byte meaning "data follows", then 1024 picture bytes, nine clocks each. Compare the 400 kHz your firmware uses with the 100 kHz of standard mode.

Step 1, count the clocks.

(1 + 1 + 1024) bytes × 9 clocks = 9234 clocks

Step 2, turn clocks into time.

400 kHz: 9234 / 400 000 ≈ 23 ms · 100 kHz: 9234 / 100 000 ≈ 92 ms

Step 3, the busy fraction. At five redraws a second, SDA is busy for 5 × 23 = 115 ms of every second, about 12 %, and released, resting at 3.3 V, the rest of the time.

For a designer: 23 ms is about one debounce window, so the screen keeps up with any press; 92 ms is a lag you would notice. The numbers also say what the wires look like: HIGH nearly always, dipping only during a redraw. A line stuck near 0 V means something holds it LOW; one drifting near 1.5 V means nothing pulls it up at all.

Example 3. A phone's 1.8 V sensor bus

Scenario. Inside a phone, the accelerometer, compass and light sensor share an I2C bus that runs at 1.8 V to save power. Most chips put HIGH at about 0.7 × VDD and LOW at about 0.3 × VDD; the ESP32-C3's 0.75 and 0.25 are a stricter version of the same rule. Suppose someone wires an OLED like yours, powered at 3.3 V, onto that 1.8 V bus.

Step 1, the phone's chips.

HIGH ≥ 0.7 × 1.8 V ≈ 1.3 V · LOW ≤ 0.3 × 1.8 V ≈ 0.5 V

Step 2, the OLED.

HIGH ≥ 0.7 × 3.3 V ≈ 2.3 V · LOW ≤ 0.3 × 3.3 V ≈ 1.0 V

Step 3, compare. The bus pull-ups go to 1.8 V, so the wire never rises above 1.8 V. To the OLED that is above its 1.0 V LOW ceiling and below its 2.3 V HIGH floor: the forbidden middle. Every 1 the phone sends is "no promise" to the OLED, which may never recognise its address. The phone's own chips are safe; nothing exceeds 1.8 V.

For a designer: a logic level is a fraction of a supply, not a fixed voltage, so two supplies on one wire need a level shifter, a small circuit with a pull-up to each rail and a transistor between them. That is why your OLED and INMP441 were chosen as 3.3 V parts: one rail, one set of thresholds, nothing to translate.

Example 4. A debounce window for a gaming keyboard

Scenario. A mechanical key switch is rated to stop bouncing within 5 ms. The keyboard's controller scans every key 1000 times a second. The fastest deliberate repeat on one key is about 20 presses a second, one every 50 ms. Pick a window and work out what it costs.

Step 1, the floor. The window must outlast the worst bounce, 5 ms. One scan is 1 ms, so "accept a new state after 5 identical scans in a row" is a 5 ms window.

Step 2, the ceiling. The window must be shorter than the 50 ms between deliberate presses, or real presses merge. 5 ms is ten times shorter, a comfortable margin.

Step 3, the cost. Every press is reported 5 ms late. A 120 Hz screen redraws every 8.3 ms, so the delay is under one frame.

5 ms bounce ≤ 5 ms window · 5 ms window is 10× shorter than the 50 ms repeat · delay added = 5 ms

For a designer: a debounce window sits between the part's bounce time and the fastest human repeat, and the scan rate sets how finely you can place it. On the Pocket AI's single action button a 20 ms window would be generous, since there is no fast repeat to protect; the button library your firmware uses defaults to a window of roughly 10 ms. On a keyboard, 20 ms would add a delay players notice.

Predict, then reveal

Thought experiment 1. A second board joins the OLED's bus, but its designer made its SDA pin push-pull instead of open-drain. During a transaction the new board drives SDA HIGH at the same moment the OLED pulls SDA LOW for an ACK. Predict what happens on the wire and inside the two chips, then reveal.

Reveal

A fight. One chip's upper switch ties the wire to 3.3 V while the other's lower switch ties it to ground, with only two closed transistors, a few ohms each, in between. The wire settles in the forbidden middle, so neither side reads a clean bit, and tens to hundreds of milliamps flow, far past the roughly 40 mA a pin can deliver at most; the push-pull chip heats and may die. Open-drain removes the upper switch, so nothing can ever push the wire up, LOW always wins, and no two devices can fight.

Thought experiment 2. The GPIO10 button's contacts have worn and now chatter for 30 ms on a slow, gentle push, instead of the 2 ms in the bounce diagram. Suppose the debounce window is the diagram's 20 ms. Predict how many presses the firmware reports for one real push, and what the fix is.

Reveal

The count becomes unpredictable. The firmware waits out the 20 ms window after the first change and reads once, but the contacts are still chattering at 20 ms, so that read may land on a 0 or a 1, and a bounce arriving after the window looks like a fresh press. One push can report one press, two, or none. A well-debounced input reports exactly one press per push; a double means the bounce outlasted the window. The fix is a longer window, say 50 ms, still far shorter than any deliberate repeat, not a different button.

Self-check 1:45–1:50

Q1. A sensor output sits at 1.5 V. What will the ESP32-C3 read?

No promise. 1.5 V is above the 0.8 V LOW ceiling and below the 2.5 V HIGH floor, so the pin may report 1, 0, or a mix that changes with noise and temperature.

Q2. Who drives SDA HIGH during an I2C transaction?

Nobody. Every device is open-drain and can only pull LOW or release. The pull-ups raise the line whenever every device has let go: the resistor on the OLED module, in parallel with the ESP32-C3's weak ~45 kΩ internal pull-up on GPIO20 and GPIO21, which the firmware enables.

Q3. What does the ninth clock after a byte carry, and who drives it?

The acknowledge slot. The sender releases SDA; the receiver pulls it LOW for ACK or leaves it HIGH for NACK. After the address byte, ACK means a device at that address is alive.

Q4. Why is the action button on GPIO10 and not on the free-looking GPIO9?

GPIO9 is a strap pin the ROM samples at reset. Held LOW during a reset it selects download mode, so a button there would make the device look dead whenever a reset happened mid-press. GPIO10 has no boot meaning.

Q5. You add a second I2C board with its own 10 kΩ pull-ups next to the OLED's 10 kΩ. What changes?

The pairs sit in parallel, so each line now has an effective 5 kΩ pull-up. Lines rise faster, but every device must sink 3.3 V / 5 kΩ ≈ 0.66 mA when LOW instead of 0.33 mA. Fine here; several more boards and a weak device's sink rating can be exceeded.

Design-your-own-device 1:50–2:00

Your own device needs an OLED, a temperature sensor (I2C, address 0x48), a NeoPixel strip (one data pin, timing-critical), and two buttons, on an ESP32-C3 with these free GPIOs: 0, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 20, 21. On paper: assign pins, decide which parts share the I2C bus, and name the two pins you would not use for anything and why.

Worked answer

I2C bus, GPIO21 SDA and GPIO20 SCL. The OLED at 0x3C and the sensor at 0x48 share the same two wires: different addresses, one bus, no extra pins. If the sensor board has its own pull-ups they parallel the OLED's, so work out the combined value.

NeoPixel data, GPIO10. The strip needs precisely timed pulses, which the ESP32-C3's RMT peripheral can route to any plain pin. GPIO10 is plain: not a strap, not USB, not needed for analog. The strip's power must not come from the 3.3 V pin; it wants its own supply, a Day 6 question.

Buttons, GPIO6 and GPIO7. Inputs with internal pull-ups, active-low, debounced in firmware. Plain pins with no boot meaning.

Leave alone: GPIO0 and GPIO1. They are the first two ADC1 channels (and the optional 32 kHz crystal pads), the pins you will want later for an analog job such as a battery-voltage divider. Nothing here needs analog, so spending them on a button would waste the chip's easiest path to reading an analog voltage. GPIO3, 4 and 5 stay spare. Straps 2, 8, 9 and USB pins 18, 19 were not offered, and that is the right instinct.
