A solar cell, one step at a time

Five pictures, short sentences. Dots are electrons. Rings are holes. The yellow arrow is light.

STEP 1

Two layers, one boundary

A solar cell is a thin slab of silicon. It is made in two layers.

The top layer has a few spare electrons. The bottom layer has a few empty spots. An empty spot is called a hole.

When the cell is made, the layers are joined. Right at the seam, a few electrons fall into holes on the other side. That happens once and then stops.

It leaves a thin strip at the seam with plus charge on top and minus charge below. That makes a built-in electric push. The push is permanent. Think of it as a one-way ramp: it shoves electrons up and holes down.

ZOOM ON STEP 1

Why a few electrons cross the seam, and why they stop

The seam is the dashed line: the surface where the top layer and the bottom layer touch. Zoom in on it. Each grey circle is one atom. In the top layer a few atoms carry a spare electron, drawn as a blue dot beside them. In the bottom layer a few atoms are missing one, drawn as a red ring: an empty spot.

Why they cross. An electron is happiest sitting in a spot, not loose beside an atom. When the layers touch, an electron next to the seam has an empty spot right across from it. It drops in, like a ball rolling into a dip in the floor. Only the electrons nearest the seam can do this; the ones further up are too far away.

What it leaves behind. The atom that lost its electron is now one short, so it is positive. The atom that gained one is now one over, so it is negative. A thin strip forms: a row of plus on the top side of the seam, a row of minus on the bottom side.

Why it stops. Like charges repel. The minus row below now pushes away any further electron that tries to come down, and the plus row above pulls it back. After the first few crossings the repulsion wins and no more electrons make it. The strip is frozen, and so is the push across it. That push is the ramp in Step 3.

STEP 2

Light knocks an electron loose

Light comes in tiny packets of energy.

When a packet hits the silicon near the seam, it kicks one electron out of its place.

Now there is a free electron. The spot it came from is a new hole.

On its own this is useless. The electron would fall back into the hole, and the energy would turn into a little heat.

STEP 3

The ramp sorts them

This is the trick that makes the cell work.

The built-in push at the seam grabs the free electron and shoves it up into the top layer. It shoves the hole down into the bottom layer. They are pulled apart before they can rejoin.

Sunlight does this trillions of times a second. The top face of the cell fills with extra electrons. The bottom face fills with holes.

The two faces are now about half a volt apart, like a weak battery. But nothing flows yet. There is nowhere to go.

STEP 4

Close the loop and it flows

Connect a wire from the top face, through your clock, to the bottom face.

Now the crowded electrons on top have a way out. They go up the wire, through the clock, and back in at the bottom. There, each one drops into a waiting hole.

That is a current. It keeps going as long as light keeps freeing electrons and the ramp keeps sorting them.

The same electrons go round and round. The cell never runs out of them. The only thing used up is the light. Cover the panel and it stops at once.

STEP 5

Into the battery

One cell gives only half a volt. So a panel is ten cells wired end to end. That adds up to 6 V.

The battery sits at 3.7 V. The panel pushes harder, so current is forced into the battery. Forcing current backwards through a battery is what charging is.

The charger board sits in between. It keeps the push at a level the cell can take. And at night, when the panel makes nothing, it closes the path so the battery cannot drain back out through the dark panel.

So, does the panel run out of electrons? No. They never leave the loop. Light lifts them, they roll down through your clock, and they come home to be lifted again. The panel is a pump, not a tank. The battery is the tank.

One more thing to be clear about. The push at the seam is an electric field, not a magnetic one. No magnets are involved. And the cell's own electrons only shuffle once, at the seam, when the cell is made. After that nothing moves until light arrives.
