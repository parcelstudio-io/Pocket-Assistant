Cyber Dawn · Phase 1 wiring

Every connection, and why

The bench alarm clock on one sheet, a short outline of each group of wires, and the Rev B parts list of 2026-10-31 (solar removed, compact body, one 16340 cell).
Pin numbers are the decided map in firmware/main/include/board_pins.h; the reasoning is docs/WIRING_CROSSREF.md and docs/REVIEW.md.

What this wiring is

Cyber Dawn's first build is a USB-powered alarm clock on a bench: one controller board, a 2.42-inch OLED display, a hardware real-time clock, a small class-D amplifier with a sealed speaker, a rotary encoder wheel, a snooze button and an arm switch, plus three sensors on the same I²C chain. Nothing here is brass yet and nothing has a battery. The wiring exists to prove one thing, that the clock wakes you with no network, before the battery goes in and the body is printed.

The sheet above groups the connections into six independent sets. Only ground is shared between them, and every set can be built and tested on its own day.

Three rules the whole sheet obeys

Every logic line is 3.3 V. The controller's pins are not 5 V tolerant. The display, the clock and every sensor run on the controller's 3V3 pin. The amplifier is the only part on 5 V, and it reads 3.3 V logic correctly because its thresholds are absolute voltages, not fractions of its supply. The sunrise lamp runs from the cell, but the controller only ever touches its switch's gate, at 3.3 V.

One power source at a time. In Phase 1 the controller's USB socket is the source and the 5V pin is an output. In Phase 4 the charger board's 5 V output, with one 16340 cell behind it, feeds the 5V pin and the controller's USB socket must be empty, or two supplies fight on the same copper. Charging then happens at the charger board's own USB-C port, never at the controller's.

The amplifier's outputs are a bridge. OUT+ and OUT− each swing between 0 and 5 V. Neither is ground, so neither may ever touch the GND rail, a scope clip or a brass part.

Two things about the stand-in

Nothing transfers, by design. Nothing from tonight is soldered to it: the chain hangs off its socket, the switches and the amp sit on jumpers. When the controller board arrives, the first QT cable moves to the QT-to-header cable on 3V3, GND, GPIO8 and GPIO9. The chain is already proven and the clock already set, so Day 1 of Phase 1 starts at the toolchain.

Its limits. A 500 mA fuse on RAW, so bench volume only; 3V feeds the chain and the OLED and nothing more; no Wi-Fi; it cannot run the ESP-IDF firmware in firmware/. If one of the two DIYmall ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1 N16R8 boards from the Amazon order of 2026-10-01 is in the drawer, use it instead of the stand-in: it has the controller board's pin map, so the controller-board variant at the end of Day Zero applies and tonight's wiring is the final wiring.

If you would rather build the whole clock on the stand-in tonight, alarm wire, amp and switches included, the sheet for that is Cyber Dawn Stand-in Wiring: the same drawing style as this page, plus the wiring laid out hole by hole on one of the 830-point breadboards. It is more than the two-hour check above; nothing on it transfers either, except the OLED header and its capacitor.

1 · The I²C chain: six boards, four wires

What. GPIO8 is SDA and GPIO9 is SCL. A QT-to-header cable turns those two pins plus 3V3 and GND into a JST SH plug, and from there the boards daisy-chain with 100 mm QT cables: rotary encoder, real-time clock, fuel gauge (Phase 4), proximity and light sensor, accelerometer, temperature and humidity sensor. No soldering anywhere in this group.

Why one bus. I²C lets many devices share two wires because each has an address. The six addresses here are all distinct, 0x49, 0x68, 0x36, 0x60, 0x18 and 0x38, so there is nothing to configure. The reference build used a motion sensor whose 0x68 collided with the clock; the accelerometer chosen here avoids that.

Why no extra pull-ups. Each Adafruit board already carries two 10 kΩ pull-ups. Six in parallel is 1.67 kΩ, which sinks 2 mA against the 3 mA limit and gives a 212 ns rise time against 300 ns at 400 kHz. The bus is legal as shipped. A seventh board would be the point to start removing pull-up jumpers.

2 · The two interrupt wires: the ones people forget

The real-time clock's SQW to GPIO4, with 10 kΩ to 3V3. This is the alarm. The clock pulls the line low when Alarm 1 matches, and GPIO4 is one of the RTC-capable pins, 0 to 21, that can wake the controller from deep sleep. The wire is not in the STEMMA QT cable; it comes from the breakout's own SQW pad. The output is open-drain, meaning the chip can only pull it down and never up, so without a resistor to 3.3 V the line floats and the alarm silently never fires. That resistor is mandatory, not a precaution.

The rotary encoder's INT to GPIO1. The encoder adapter pulses its INT pin low on any turn or press, also open-drain. This line does not have to be valid before firmware runs, so the controller's internal pull-up is enough, but it must be enabled in code or the wheel will seem to work only when polled.

The accelerometer's INT to GPIO5 comes in Phase 3 for tap-to-wake. GPIO5 was chosen because it is RTC-capable; the September map had this on GPIO40, which cannot wake anything. Unlike the other two, this output is push-pull and active-high by default (its polarity is a register bit), so it needs no pull-up, and the wake must be set for a high level or the bit flipped to match.

3 · The display: seven wires, no level shifter

What. The OLED display's 20-pin header is marked by number: pin 2 (VCC) to 3V3, pin 1 (GND) to GND, then five signal lines: pin 7 (SCLK) on GPIO12, pin 8 (MOSI) on GPIO11, pin 15 (CS) on GPIO10, pin 4 (DC) on GPIO6, pin 16 (RESET) on GPIO7. Pins 3, 5, 6, 9–14 and 17–19 stay empty, and pin 20, the metal frame, may float. The kit's 220 µF goes across pins 1 and 2 at the module, stripe to pin 1.

Why these pins. GPIO10 to 13 are the controller's FSPI pins routed through the IO mux, the fastest path for a 128×64 framebuffer refresh. DC and RESET are plain outputs and can be anywhere; 6 and 7 are free and not strapping pins.

Why the shifter stays in the bag. The level shifter in the box exists for 5 V boards like an Arduino Uno. The controller is already 3.3 V, so wiring it in would add an IC and twelve joints for nothing. The 220 µF stays because the module wants a stiff 3.3 V rail; the controller's regulator is 50 mm away.

Before wiring. The module picks parallel, SPI or I²C with two 0 Ω links on its back, BS1 and BS2. The first board shipped in 8080 parallel (photo of 2026-11-03), so move R2 to R3 and R4 to R5, which is Adafruit's SPI setting. Take each upper link off before its lower pads are bridged: both fitted short 3.3 V to ground. The steps and meter checks are in Day Zero's Optional A; then solder the header.

4 · Audio: four signals, 5 V, and a hardware ceiling

What. Into the amplifier: BCLK on GPIO14, LRC on GPIO15, DIN on GPIO16, SD on GPIO17. VIN from the controller's 5V pin, GND to GND, 220 µF and 100 nF across them at the amp. GAIN strapped to VIN. The speaker on OUT+ and OUT−.

Why 5 V and not 3.3. Not for loudness: with GAIN strapped to VIN, 3.3 V would still give about 1.4 W against 1.6 W on 5 V. The reason is heat. On 3.3 V the amp's current would pass through the controller's linear regulator, which is already the hottest part on the board.

Why GAIN to VIN. That strap selects 6 dB instead of the default 9 dB and caps the output near 1.6 W into 4 Ω. At 9 dB the amp's 1.3 A peaks exceed the 1 A boost converter that arrives in Phase 4. Putting the ceiling in copper means no firmware bug can raise it.

Why SD has a pull-down. The breakout has a 1 MΩ pull-up from SD to VIN, so the amp powers up enabled. A 10 kΩ to ground wins against that and holds the amp silent until GPIO17 is driven, so a reset mid-alarm cannot leave the speaker screaming on noise. Driven high by GPIO17, the amp plays the left channel only, so the firmware puts the mono stream in the left I²S slot.

Why the capacitors sit at the pins. A bass note asks for hundreds of milliamps in a millisecond. The electrolytic answers the slow part, the ceramic the fast part, and every centimetre of wire between them and the amp makes them slower.

5 · Snooze, arm, and power

Snooze button on GPIO18, arm switch on GPIO21, each switch's other leg to GND. Both pins are RTC-capable so a press can wake the sleeping clock, and the controller's internal pull-ups make them read high until pressed. In deep sleep those internal pull-ups work only while the RTC peripheral domain stays powered, so the firmware keeps it on (or 10 kΩ pull-ups go on the board) if deep sleep is ever used. The arm switch is a logic input only; a clock you can power off is not a reliable alarm.

Power in Phase 1 is the USB socket. Days 1 to 3 run from any port. From Day 4 the amplifier adds about 360 mA average and 0.9 A peaks to the controller's 565 mA, so use a USB-C wall adapter rated 2 A. A laptop port browns out and the symptom looks exactly like a wiring fault.

Power in Phase 4 is one 16340 cell (Rev B, 2026-10-31; no solar). The owned 16340 holder with leads goes on a JST-PH pigtail through the fuel gauge into the charger board's BAT port, jumper cut to 500 mA (it ships closed at 1 A). The charger board's 5 V boost output feeds the 5V pin and the amplifier VIN; the charger's own USB-C port is the charging port; its solar input stays empty. Meter the holder leads for polarity before the cell meets anything.

Why the controller's regulator sets the rules. It is an 800 mA low-dropout linear regulator (per the controller board's schematic; an earlier note assumed a 1.1 V-dropout part) with about 0.3 V of dropout at this load: the 5 V rail must stay above about 3.7 V or the 3.3 V rail collapses and the controller resets. That is the brownout mechanism Phase 4 will scope while the clock runs unplugged on the cell, and it is why nothing else may be hung on the controller's 3V3 pin beyond the display and the chain.

6 · The sunrise lamp: one filament, one switch (added 2026-11-05)

What. An LED filament lies in a 40 × 2 mm slot along the lid's front edge. Its + lead takes the cell's + through 15 Ω; its − lead goes to the drain of a MOSFET switch whose source is on GND. GPIO40 drives the gate through 100 Ω, with 10 kΩ from gate to GND. On the bench, before the cell is in, the + side comes from the 5V pin through 33 Ω.

Why the cell and not the 5 V boost. The boost is rated 1 A and an alarm already peaks near 0.93 A. The cell allows 2 A, so the lamp's 0.1 A comes from the cell's + lead, spliced into the holder's pigtail, and the boost budget is untouched. Plugged in, the charger feeds that same node.

Why a resistor, and why its current drifts. An LED's current climbs steeply with voltage, so the resistor sets it: about 0.1 A on a full 4.2 V cell, 0.06 A at 3.7 V. The firmware reads the cell voltage from the fuel gauge and scales the dimming so the sunrise looks the same on any charge.

Why a MOSFET. A GPIO pin gives about 20 mA; the lamp wants five times that. The switch's threshold is 1.45 V at most, so 3.3 V turns it fully on, and the 10 kΩ holds it off while the controller boots. GPIO40 is free in both encoder options and needs no wake ability: it is an output.

How the sunrise runs. The clock's Alarm 2 fires 30 minutes before the alarm, on the same SQW wire, and wakes the controller. The lamp fades up on a perceptual curve, then Alarm 1 rings as before. About 25 lumens at full: a warm glow on the nightstand, not a daylight lamp. A morning's sunrise costs about 2 % of the cell.

Before power, with the meter

  • 3V3 to GND, 5V to GND, 5V to 3V3: no steady beep. A chirp that stops is a capacitor.
  • OUT+ and OUT− to GND and to each other: silent, or a few ohms through the speaker only.
  • Clock SQW to 3V3: about 10 kΩ. If it reads open, the pull-up is missing and the alarm will never fire.
  • Amp SD to GND: about 10 kΩ. If it reads near 1 MΩ or open, the pull-down is missing.
  • Lamp switch gate to GND: about 10 kΩ. Filament − to GND: open until GPIO40 drives the gate.
  • Every display signal pin to its controller pin: beep. Adjacent display pins to each other: silent.
  • The controller's USB socket empty whenever a bench supply or, later, the charger board feeds the 5V pin.
  • Phase 4: the 16340 holder's leads, red to the + pad, before the cell goes in. Red on the wrong pad is the one mistake no protection circuit undoes.
