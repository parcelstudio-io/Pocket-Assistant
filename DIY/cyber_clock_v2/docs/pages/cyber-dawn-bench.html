Cyber Dawn · Phase 1 bench build

Five Hours to an Alarm Clock

Days 1–5 · one hour each · USB power only
At the end you have a working real-time-clock alarm with sound and snooze. No display, no network, no battery.
Before day 1

Place the core order and wait for it. These five sessions assume the bench BOM is on your desk. Nothing here needs the cell, the charger board or the brass cradles — those wait for the Phase 4 and Phase 6 gates (Rev B, 2026-10-31: no solar).

Everything runs from the USB cable. There is no lithium cell in this build, so there is no fire risk to manage yet. Keep it that way until the power design is measured.

What you are building

Five sessions, each one hour, each ending with something that works. You are not assembling the product — you are proving the one thing it exists to do: wake up on time without a network. Everything else is added later, on top of a foundation you have already tested.

DAY 1

Toolchain and first light

1 HOUR · NO SOLDERING

Goal

A board that boots your code and prints to your screen. Nothing is wired to it yet.

On the bench

Controller board · USB-C data cable · your computer.

Steps

  1. Read the silkscreen and write down the revision. Look near the USB connectors for v1.1 or v1.0. This matters: the onboard RGB LED is on GPIO38 on v1.1 and GPIO48 on v1.0. Your pin map avoids both, but you want to know which board you have.
  2. Install ESP-IDF 5.x and pin the version. Record the exact version in the repo — a floating toolchain will waste a Saturday later.
  3. Gate the board on its flash size before trusting it: 
    esptool.py --port /dev/ttyACM0 flash_id
    You need 8 MB. Anything smaller is a mislabelled clone — set it aside and use another.
  4. Build and flash the scaffold that is already in the repo: 
    cd firmware
idf.py set-target esp32s3
idf.py build
idf.py -p /dev/ttyACM0 flash monitor

Done when

The monitor prints Cyber Dawn firmware scaffold booted, a free-heap number, and then a heartbeat line once a minute. That is the whole goal. Close the laptop.

If it doesn't work

  • No serial port at all. Nine times out of ten the cable is charge-only. Try a different one before anything else. Check with ls /dev/ttyACM* /dev/ttyUSB*.
  • Port exists, flashing fails. Hold the BOOT button, tap RESET, release BOOT, then flash. This forces download mode by hand.
  • Permission denied on Linux. Add yourself to the dialout group and log out and back in.
  • Flash reports under 8 MB. Do not continue with that board. The partition table needs the full 8 MB.
DAY 2

The I²C chain

1 HOUR · NO SOLDERING

Goal

Six sensor boards answering on two wires. This is the most satisfying hour of the five and you will not touch the iron.

On the bench

1 × QT-to-header cable · 5 × QT cables · real-time clock · coin cell · fuel gauge · Adafruit proximity and light sensor · accelerometer · temperature and humidity sensor · rotary encoder.

Steps

  1. Push the coin cell into the real-time clock, printed + side facing up. Without it the clock forgets the time every time you unplug.
  2. Plug the QT-to-header cable's male ends into the controller board: red → 3V3, black → GND, blue → GPIO8, yellow → GPIO9.
  3. Plug its JST end into either QT port on the real-time clock. The two ports on each board are wired together — there is no in or out.
  4. Daisy-chain the other five boards with the QT cables, in any order.
  5. Flash a scan that walks addresses 0x08 to 0x77 and logs every device that acknowledges.

Done when

Six addresses appear: 0x18 (or 0x19), 0x36, 0x38, 0x49, 0x60, 0x68. Write the list into the repo — it is your baseline for every later fault.

If it doesn't work

  • Nothing at all responds. SDA and SCL are swapped, or the red wire is not on 3V3. Check the colours against the diagram before suspecting a board.
  • One address missing. That board or the cable feeding it. Swap the cable first, it is the cheaper test.
  • Addresses appear and vanish between scans. Too many pull-ups in parallel. Each breakout brings its own 10 kΩ; six in parallel is about 1.7 kΩ. Measure SDA to 3V3 with the power off — if it reads under 1 kΩ, remove the pull-up jumper on some boards.
  • An unexpected address appears. Note it. Some boards carry a second chip; nothing in this set conflicts, but you want to know it is there.
DAY 3

The alarm wire

1 HOUR · FIRST SOLDER
This is the important hour

Everything else in this product is a feature. This one wire is the product. If it is wrong, the clock does not wake you — and it fails silently, which is worse.

Goal

The real-time clock pulls a pin low at a time you chose, with no code running in between.

On the bench

Yesterday's chain, still plugged in · soldering iron · a 10 kΩ resistor · two short hookup wires.

Steps

  1. Find the pad labelled INT/SQW along the breakout's edge. It is not carried on the QT connectors — this is the connection people miss.
  2. Solder a wire to it. Tin the pad first, then the wire, then join them; that is three easy joins instead of one hard one.
  3. Run that wire to GPIO4.
  4. Solder the 10 kΩ from the same node up to 3V3.
  5. Measure before you trust it. Power up and put a meter on GPIO4. It must read about 3.3 V at rest. If it reads 0 V, stop — the pull-up is missing or you are on the wrong pad.
  6. Write UTC into the clock, set Alarm 1 for two minutes ahead, and poll GPIO4 in a loop while logging.

Done when

GPIO4 sits at 3.3 V, then drops to 0 V at the minute you programmed, and your log prints it. Reset the board mid-wait and confirm it still fires — the clock does not care that the controller rebooted, and proving that is the entire point.

If it doesn't work

  • GPIO4 reads 0 V at rest. No pull-up, or the wire is on a ground pad. Check with the meter, not by eye.
  • It never goes low. Either the alarm registers are wrong, or a previous alarm flag was never cleared — the clock holds the line low until you clear it, so a stale flag looks like a dead alarm on the next run.
  • It fires immediately. You wrote a time in the past. The clock stores UTC, not your local time; mixing the two by a few hours is the classic mistake here.
DAY 4

Making a noise

1 HOUR · SOLDERING

Goal

A clean tone from the speaker, generated in code, with a hardware ceiling on how loud it can ever get.

On the bench

Amplifier · header strip · one enclosed 4 Ω speaker of the stereo pair · iron · hookup wire.

The one that will surprise you

The breakout carries a 1 MΩ resistor from SD to VIN. That means the amplifier powers up enabled by default. If GPIO17 is left as a floating input at boot, the amp wakes up live and you get a pop — or worse, noise at full volume.

Set GPIO17 as an output driven LOW before you enable anything else. That ordering is already written into the project's boot sequence; today is when it starts mattering.

Steps

  1. Solder the header strip to the amplifier. Seat it flat — tack one end pin, check it sits square, then do the rest.
  2. Strap GAIN to VIN with a short wire. This selects 6 dB instead of the 9 dB default and is the ceiling that keeps peak current inside what the battery boost can supply in Phase 4.
  3. Wire the six connections from the diagram.
  4. Connect the speaker across OUT+ and OUT−. Neither lead touches ground, now or ever.
  5. In firmware: drive GPIO17 low, bring up I²S, generate a tone in code (not from a file), then raise GPIO17 to enable the amp.

Done when

A clean, steady tone. Pulling GPIO17 low silences it instantly. No pop at boot, and no hiss when idle.

If it doesn't work

  • Silence. Check GPIO17 is actually high — it is the enable, and it is the most common cause.
  • Buzzing or crackle rather than a tone. I²S clock settings do not match what you are feeding it, or BCLK and LRC are swapped.
  • A pop every time it boots. GPIO17 is not being driven low early enough. Move it earlier in your init.
  • Very quiet. That may be correct — you deliberately chose 6 dB. Judge it again once the speaker is in a sealed box; a bare driver on a desk sounds far weaker than a mounted one.
DAY 5

A clock that wakes you

1 HOUR · LIGHT SOLDERING

Goal

Put the four pieces together: the clock fires, the tone plays, a button snoozes it, a switch disarms it.

Steps

  1. Snooze button: one leg to GPIO18, the other to GND.
  2. Arm switch: one leg to GPIO21, the other to GND.
  3. Enable the internal pull-up on both pins in firmware. No external resistors on the breadboard.
  4. Wire the behaviour together:
    • GPIO4 falls → start the tone
    • Snooze pressed → silence, set the clock for one minute ahead, clear the flag
    • Arm switch off → cancel everything and clear the flag
  5. Debounce the button in software — contacts chatter for several milliseconds and will otherwise register many presses.

Done when

Set an alarm two minutes out. It rings. You press snooze, it stops, and one minute later it rings again. Flip the arm switch and it stays quiet.

Then pull the USB cable mid-ring, plug it back in, and confirm the snooze survived. That is the reset test, and passing it is the moment you actually have an alarm clock.

What you have after five hours

  • A board you can build and flash reliably, with its revision recorded.
  • Six sensors on two wires, with a known-good address baseline.
  • A hardware clock that fires an interrupt whether or not your code is running.
  • Audio with a ceiling set in hardware, and no boot pop.
  • Snooze and arm, surviving a reset.

No display. No Wi-Fi. No battery. That is deliberate: every one of those can fail without stopping the alarm, and you have now proved the alarm works before adding anything that could get in its way.
