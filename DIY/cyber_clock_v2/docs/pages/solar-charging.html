Charging a cell from the sun

Eight pictures: four for the day, two for the night, the two curves, and open versus shorted. Follows Solar Cell Steps. Blue dots are electrons, green dots are lithium ions.
NOTE · 2026-10-31
Teaching page, kept. Cyber Dawn dropped solar on 2026-10-31 (Rev B: the clock charges over USB-C and runs on one 16340 cell between unplugs; the 5 W panel is owned stock for another project). Pictures 4 to 6 and the two curves still describe the charger board in use: the same current limit, 4.2 V stop and night-time hand-over, with the board's own USB-C port in place of the panel. 500 mA is the charger jumper setting for the 16340 cell.

First, the question itself. Yes, current flows through the battery while it charges. Electrons go in at one end and out at the other, at the same rate, the whole time. The battery does not fill up with electrons. It fills up with moved lithium. The pictures show how.

PICTURE 1

It is still one loop

The cell has two wires, like everything else. Electrons from the panel go in at the cell's minus end and come out at its plus end. From there they go back to the panel.

Nothing has only one wire, so nothing can fill up with electrons. What goes in comes out. Charging happens inside the cell, while the electrons pass through.

PICTURE 2

Inside: lithium moves, electrons pass

A lithium cell is two plates with a thin wall between them. The wall lets lithium ions through but blocks electrons.

When the panel pushes electrons into the minus plate, that plate becomes more negative. Lithium ions are positive, so they are pulled across the wall to join the electrons and park in the graphite. To keep things balanced, an electron leaves the plus plate for every ion that leaves it. That is the electron coming out of the other wire.

Lithium parked in graphite is like a lifted weight. It wants to go back. That wish is the stored energy. At night it goes back, and pushes electrons through your clock on the way.

PICTURE 3

Why it flows into the cell at all

Voltage is a level, like water in a tank. Current only flows from a higher level to a lower one.

The panel sits at about 6 V. The cell sits at 3.7 V, rising to 4.2 V when full. The panel is higher, so it pushes current into the cell. If you tried it the other way round, a 3.7 V panel into a 4.2 V cell, nothing would flow.

But a bare pipe would be too much. The charger board is the valve on it.

PICTURE 4

What the charger board does

Four jobs. It limits the current so the cell charges gently, about 500 mA for your cell. It stops raising the level at 4.2 V, because a lithium cell above that is unsafe. It shuts the path at night, when the panel's level falls below the cell's, so the cell cannot drain backwards into the dark panel. And it feeds the clock directly at the same time, so the clock runs from the sun by day and from the cell by night.

That is the whole chain. Light frees electrons in the panel. The panel's higher level pushes them around the loop. The charger meters them. They pass through the cell and move lithium as they go. The moved lithium is the stored energy. The electrons come home.

PICTURE 5

Night: the cell takes over

When the light goes, the panel's level drops to nothing and the charger shuts the door to it. The panel is out of the picture until morning.

Now the cell is the highest level in the system, so it pushes. Electrons leave its minus end, go through the charger board's output, through the clock, and come back in at the plus end. It is the same loop as the day, run backwards, with the cell as the pump instead of the sun.

The charger board does not do anything clever here. Its output is simply wired to the cell, so the clock sees the cell's voltage, which is what it runs on.

PICTURE 6

Inside at night: the lithium goes home

The lithium parked in the graphite wants the snug spots in the oxide plate back. One by one the ions cross the wall to the plus plate.

But the oxide can only take an ion back if it also gets an electron back, and electrons cannot cross the wall. So for each ion that goes home, one electron has to go the long way: out of the minus plate, through the wire, through the clock, and into the plus plate. The lithium's wish to go home is what pushes that electron around the loop. That push is the 3.7 V.

When the last lithium has gone home, the wish is gone, the push is gone, and the cell is flat. Then the sun comes up, the panel's level rises above the cell's, and Pictures 1 to 4 start again.

THE TWO CURVES

Current against voltage

The panel is not a fixed source. Short its wires and it gives its full current, about 0.9 A for your panel, at zero volts. Leave the wires open and it sits at about 7 V giving no current. In between the curve is flat and then drops at a knee. The knee, about 6 V and 0.8 A, is where volts times amps is biggest: the 5 W on the label. A good charger pulls the panel to its knee. Less light moves the whole curve down (less current) but barely left (same voltage), which is why a shaded panel still shows 6 V on a meter and still cannot run anything.

The cell is charged in two stages, and the charger board switches between them on its own. First, constant current: the board pushes a fixed 500 mA in while the cell's voltage climbs from about 3.0 V to 4.2 V. That is most of the charge. Then, constant voltage: the board holds 4.2 V and the cell takes less and less current as it fills, tapering from 500 mA down. When the current falls to about a tenth of the setting, around 50 mA, the board calls it full and stops. Never let a lithium cell past 4.2 V; the taper is how it is filled safely.

WHAT SHORTING LOOKS LIKE

Open, shorted, working

Open means the two wires touch nothing. The panel is lit, electrons pile up on one face and holes on the other, and the meter across the wires reads the full 7 V. But no loop, so no current.

Shorted means a bare wire joins the two wires directly, with nothing in between. Now every electron that light frees runs straight around and back in, with nothing to push against, so the voltage collapses to about zero and the current is as high as the light allows, about 0.9 A. Unlike a shorted battery, this does not hurt a panel: the light sets how many electrons there are, and the wire just lets them all go.

Working means something is in the loop that resists a little, like the clock. The flow is nearly as big, about 0.8 A, but the faces stay charged at about 6 V because the clock holds the electrons back. The knee on the curve is this point: the most volts times amps the panel can give.
