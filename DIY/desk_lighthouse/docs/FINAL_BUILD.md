## The decisions

### A lighthouse drawn in four lines

Four 1.5 mm brass rods run from the coaster to the roof: Ø54 at the feet, leaning in 4.3° to Ø29 where they pass the gallery, then straight up as the lantern's bars to the roof, which three of them carry. No rings, no braces, no glass, no house: the coaster holds their feet, the deck holds their waists, the roof their tips, and a 1.5 mm brass rod fixed at both ends carries the lantern's 67 g with a buckling margin of about 211 to one. Up the middle the 4 mm spine turns. 243 mm tall, about 217 g before the ballast. Rev A's tube and cube, Rev B's screen and Rev C's glass and house are gone; what is left is a wire drawing of a lighthouse with a lamp in it.

### The lamp stands still and the brass turns round it

A real lighthouse does not spin its lamp; the lens turns round a fixed light. Here the lamp is a 26 mm LED filament standing on the axis, and a brass drum with a 120° window turns round it, one revolution in 7.5 s: a 2.5 s sweep of light across the room, then brass. No slip rings, no wire ever moves. When rotation is off, the window parks facing you and the lantern is a lamp. When the lamp is off, the shutter closes and shows you brass.

### On a cell: the battery stands at the foot

The builder asked for a battery and a charger below (2026-11-09). The battery is the family's cell, a Nitecore 16340 of which thirteen are in the drawer, in the owned holder, standing on end on the coaster's top behind the motor, in the open: 18 × 17 × 43 mm of black tray, the one dark block in the build, a colour the rule allows. The cell lifts out of its tray; nothing is soldered to it. 129 mA from the cell with the beacon turning and the lamp at 70 %: about 7 hours of lighthouse, 8 of lamp alone, 2 days asleep with the boost off. The cell's voltage comes to the controller through two 100 kΩ, so it dims, parks and shuts down in order instead of dying mid-turn.

### The charger in the coaster, the ports at the back

The same charger board as Cyber Dawn and the satellite, a bq25185 with a 5 V boost: USB-C in, 500 mA into the cell with its jumper cut (2.3 h from empty), a power path that runs the lighthouse from the cable while it charges, and a 1 A boost that makes the 5 V rail for the motor and the lamp. The controller takes the cell straight through an SS14, the satellite's trick, so the boost can sleep: GPIO7 wakes it only while something moves. Its terminal block and JST socket come off and it lies 4.8 mm flat in the coaster's hollow beside the controller, both ports in one 40 mm notch at the back of the rim: the charging cable and, rarely, the flashing cable. That notch is the build's one compromise.

### The motor on the coaster, the spine up the middle

A wire frame has nowhere to hide a motor at the top, so the motor stands at the foot on a bed of hot glue, shaft up on the axis, and the owned 4 mm brass tube slides over its D shaft and runs 148 mm up through the deck's bearing to the shutter. The bearing is a wheel made of what is already here: a 3 mm ring of the 5/8 in tube glued on the deck over its 11 mm hole, two 1 mm spokes, and a 3 mm ring of the owned 5 mm tube as the hub, which the 4 mm spine telescopes into with about 0.1 mm of play. It is soldered up on the spine itself, so it centres itself, then slid down onto the deck. The spine is the one line beyond the outline, and it is the one that moves: the lighthouse's own lamp post, turning.

### A reed switch so it knows where the window is

A DC gearmotor has no idea where it stopped. A 1 mm brass rod 6.5 mm long is soldered to the spine as an arm, 6.5 mm above the gearbox, with a 3 × 1 mm magnet glued under its tip, 7 mm out, placed opposite the shutter's window; a glass reed switch stands behind the motor, in front of the cell, with its top 2 mm under the magnet's path, so it closes once a revolution at the moment the window faces front. From that one pulse the firmware times each revolution and trims the PWM to 7.5 s, parks the window on you when you turn rotation off, closes the shutter by running on half a turn when the lamp goes off, and notices a jam. Two short wires; nothing climbs the tower for it.

### Two of the four rods are the lamp's wires

Nothing may climb a wire frame but the frame, so the lamp's current goes up one rod and comes down another. The back-left rod is live: white heat-shrink where it passes the coaster and the deck, a white boot on its tip, a wire soldered to its foot in the hollow and another from its tip to the filament's + tab under the roof; it is the one rod that stops short of the roof. The back-right rod is the return, soldered to a ground wire at its foot and to the nut's wire at its tip, and through the roof like the two front rods. 8.1 mΩ each; the brass does not notice 83 mA. The rover's frame is its ground; this is the same idea with one rod insulated, and the meter checks it before power.

### A lamp post that is also the return

A rigid LED filament has a metal tab at each end, the + one with a hole in it. So a 1 mm brass rod is soldered 5 mm up inside the nipple's hollow bore, runs down behind the lamp at r 3.1 inside the shutter, bends forward at the bottom and is soldered to the lower tab: it holds the filament rigid on the axis and carries its return to the nut. The + tab at the top takes one wire. Inside the drum a glowing strip and a thin brass post behind it, the way a lamp sits in a real lantern.

### Warm white, 2700 K, not the family's amber

The hologram and the satellite use a 2200 K filament as a pool of amber on brass, and eight of them are spare. A lighthouse lamp is not a pool: it is the light itself, and the rule is white or warm white. A 26 mm, 3 V, 100 mA filament at 2700 K is an incandescent lamp's colour; 83 mA through 23.5 Ω from the 5 V rail, 249 mW, dimmed to 70 % by day. Its phosphor is yellow when off, which is why the shutter closes over it.

### Lamp-maker's parts as architecture

### One owned driver board does both jobs

The camera Pocket AI's spare MX1508 dual H-bridge: channel A is the shutter's motor with speed, brake and the parking move; channel B, with one input tied low, is a 1.5 A high-side switch for the lamp through its resistors, the lamp returning through the frame. Both loads get the boost's 5 V, the controller's GPIOs see only logic, and there are no MOSFETs, gate resistors or flyback diodes to wire. Charger board pads used: the battery pads where the JST socket was (the tray's leads), `BAT` (to the diode and the divider), `5V` (the boost, to the driver), `EN` (from GPIO7), `G` (ground), the USB-C port (charging); not used: `VIN`, `VU`, `D+`, `D−`, the terminal block (removed); the charge-rate jumper on the back cut for 500 mA. ## The budget

## What the firmware needs

- ESP-IDF 6 for the esp32c3, a new small project beside the hologram's. No Wi-Fi: the console on USB Serial/JTAG, the button the whole interface.
- GPIO7 high to wake the boost before anything moves, low again when the shutter is parked and the lamp is off; the boost stalls over 200 mA at switch-on, so the motor's PWM ramps from 0 to its duty over 0.2 s and the lamp fades up after it.
- LEDC: GPIO0 at 20 kHz for the motor (silent), GPIO3 at 5 kHz for the lamp. GPIO1 low for forward, high with GPIO0 high to brake. All three driven low first thing at boot.
- The reed on GPIO6, interrupt on the falling edge, 5 ms debounce: period of the last revolution; a PI loop trims the motor duty to hold 7.5 s; no pulse for twice that with the motor on = jammed, motor off, boost off.
- Parking: on rotation off, run until the reed closes, then brake: the window faces front (the magnet sits opposite the window, over the reed behind the motor). On lamp off, run from the index for half a period, then brake: the shutter is closed. On lamp on, open again. A small offset in NVS trims the park angle after the first bench run.
- The button on GPIO10: short press rotation on/off; long press (1 s) lamp off, low, full. The lamp blinks once to say so.
- The cell on GPIO4 through the divider, read once a minute: below 3.5 V the lamp drops to low and blinks twice; below 3.3 V the shutter parks, the lamp goes out, the boost goes off, and the controller sleeps until the button or a higher reading.
- First-power measurements the record wants: the current from the cell in each mode (expected about 129 mA beacon, 112 lamp, 20 asleep), the lamp's current at full (about 83 mA), the revolution period from the reed, and the charge current with the jumper cut (about 500 mA).

## Before power, with the meter

- Before the cell meets anything: the holder's leads for polarity on the meter with the cell in (3.6 to 4.1 V, button top +); the charger's jumper cut; the filament's polarity with the diode test (it lights faintly; the tab with the hole is +).
- With the boards wired on the bench, the cell in and the lantern not yet up: the cell's voltage on the charger's BAT pad, 3.5 to 4.0 V on the controller's 5V pin (the diode's drop), 5.0 V on the driver's VCC with GPIO7 high and nothing with it low, half the cell's voltage on GPIO4; then the lamp channel at full gives 2.9 to 3.1 V across the filament, about 83 mA, the motor channel turns the spine, the brake stops it, and the reed closes once a turn. Plug the charger's cable: the orange LED, and the lighthouse still runs.
- The live rod alone: back-left rod to the coaster open, to the deck open, to the roof open, before its wires go on. Then back-right rod's foot to the nut: a short, through the solder at both ends. Nothing else bare against brass: the motor's tabs, the reed's leads, the holder's leads and both wires under the roof in heat-shrink. Everything else is a supplier spec and is not metered.

## Rules this build follows

- Design first: sleek, few lines (four rods and a spine; no rings, no braces, no glass, no house); wires in the hollow, two of them the rods themselves; no closed surface at all.
- Colour: brass and warm white; black only in the pad under the coaster, the cell's wrap and its tray, in the open at the foot as the rule allows; the boards in the hollow; the filament's yellow phosphor hidden by the shutter when off; white heat-shrink where a sleeve shows.
- No clear acrylic and no folded brass sheet, at the builder's word of 2026-11-09 evening: the lantern is open and the three small brass pieces are rod and tube.
- Power drawn on both sheets: the cell stands where the form sheet shows it, and the wiring sheet reads cell, charger, controller, driver, loads.
- Component names with the link on the word; no product codes in prose.
- Owned parts stay off the shopping list: the cell, the tray, the diode, the resistors, the controller and the driver are family stock, the rods and the tubes are shared stock.
- Every wire on the sheet is traceable pin to pin; the generator checks clearance and counts crossings (zero).
- Brass at the builder's word for this build (2026-11-07); the family's rule since the drone is stainless.
- Meter only what matters: three checks before power, one of them the live rod against every other piece of brass, one the tray's polarity before the cell meets anything.
