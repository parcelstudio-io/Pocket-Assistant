What it is

A glass cube on a low brass base, with a 2.42-inch display hidden inside the brass. Press the button, ask a question, and the answer appears as white text floating inside the cube, over a dim amber pool of light on the desk from the LED filament already in the drawer. The microphone's audio goes over Wi-Fi to the desktop, where the voice server that already runs for the first Pocket AI turns it into words (Whisper) and an answer (Ollama) and sends the text back.

The reference is Raspduino Uno's ESP32 OLED Hologram Lyric Video Display (August 2026, a million views): an ESP32 plays lyric frames on a 0.96-inch OLED under a beam-splitter cube. Its description names exactly three parts, and the cube is the whole trick. Ours is the same trick, four times the picture, plus ears and a brain.

This page is step 1 of the house rule: the final build first. One form sheet, one wiring sheet, every part and every pin. Rev A was approved on 2026-11-01 and its physics page followed. Rev B (2026-11-07) dressed the module in a painted brass-sheet skin and was never approved. Rev C (2026-11-10) is up for approval: the base is brass, a frame of square brass tube round the glass with an apron of brass strip and four legs of the owned brass rod. No paint, no skin, no ivory; the wiring is untouched. Nothing is cut or soldered until it is approved.

The decisions

A cube, not a tilted sheet

The video's trick is a 50:50 beam-splitter cube sitting over the display. Half the light from every pixel reflects off the hidden diagonal inside the glass and leaves through the front face, so the text appears upright, inside the cube, at its back face. A 45° acrylic sheet does the same for $5 but needs a frame to hold it, a black box around it, and gives a double image from its two surfaces. The cube is one part, no ghost, 314 g of glass that stays put.

50 mm: the largest cube that ships at a sane price

The owned 2.42" display has 55 × 27.5 mm of pixels. A 50 mm cube covers 50 × 27.5 of that: 116 of 128 columns and all 64 rows. The picture hangs 11 mm above the cube's base and 39 mm below its top. At 0.43 mm a pixel, a 16-pixel line of text is 7 mm tall: three lines of 14 characters, or eight lines of 19 at the small font. Long answers page. Bigger was asked for on 2026-11-01. Nothing is sold between 50 and 70 mm, and a 70 mm cube shows the same pixels inside more glass: the display, not the cube, sets the picture. Decided the same evening: 50 mm stays.

The spare 2.42" display, face up under the cube

Cyber Dawn bought two; its wiring page already proved the pin jumpers, the 220 µF and the 4 MHz SPI limit on paper. Black pixels send nothing into the cube, which is why an OLED and not an LCD. The firmware mirrors the columns, so the bare display reads backwards and the reflection reads right. Its board is the green one the colour rule names, so the board goes where nobody sees it: under the brass.

A brass base: a frame of square tube, an apron of strip (Rev C, 2026-11-10)

On 2026-11-10 the builder asked for brass in place of the ivory. The rules written since Rev B rule its skin out three times: no paint, no cover over a part, no folded brass sheet. So the brass is the frame itself, in bar stock. Four lengths of 1/4 in square brass tube make a rectangle round the display's glass. The numbers fall out of the parts: the opening is the glass plus 0.25 mm a side, the bars are wide enough to roof the whole board, and the frame comes out 50.2 mm deep under a 50 mm cube. The cube sits on the front and back bars. The display's glass rides 2.6 mm below it and carries nothing, and the two bars show in the glass as a brass band above and below the text. A 1/2 in brass strip, soldered flat to the frame's four outside faces, hangs 6.3 mm below it: the apron. It hides the board's edge, the header, the controller, the microphone and all seventeen wires, and stops 6.8 mm above the desk, where the amber light comes out. From any side: glass, a 12.7 mm band of brass, light. 75 × 52 mm, about 39 g, twelve solder joints, all brass to brass, all made before the electronics come near. The strip is bar stock used flat, not a folded skin. If the apron still reads as a cover, leave it off: the open tube frame stands alone, with the board's edge in its shadow.

Four straight brass legs, socketed in the frame

Four 19 mm lengths of the owned 1.5 mm brass rod, no bends. Each goes up a 1/16 in hole in a side bar's underside, stops against the inside of the tube's top wall and is soldered at the hole. The frame and its legs are one brass table; the module is no longer the structure. The sockets are marked through the module's own mounting holes, so afterwards the module slides up the legs, sleeved in heat-shrink, and a drop of gel glue at each hole holds it 1.5 mm under the bars. 6.8 mm of each leg shows under the apron. The legs stand 37 mm apart front to back and the weight sits 38 mm up, so it tips at 26°; Rev B's bent feet gave 34°. Pushed anywhere below the cube's top edge it slides before it tips. That is the price of four plain points, and the physics page takes it up after approval.

ESP32-C3 SuperMini, not an ESP32-S3 DevKit

Thirteen are owned, it is 22.5 × 18 mm, and the first Pocket AI's voice firmware already streams this microphone to the desktop from this exact board. Nine pins are needed; the C3 has thirteen. GPIO2, 8 and 9 stay empty, so no strapping-pin resistor. The one risk Rev C adds: the controller's antenna now sits inside a brass ring. The ring is open underneath by the slot all round, and the board already runs at a capped 8.5 dBm, but the signal is read at first power, frame on and frame off, before anything is glued.

Push to talk, no wake word, no speaker

A button on GPIO10 streams while held and takes the turn on release, the first Pocket AI's model: no always-on microphone, no wake-word model on a C3. The button stands on the frame's front right corner, pressed straight down onto the legs, black like the glass. The answer is text in the glass; the server's spoken reply is ignored.

An amber glow under the brass, from the filament already owned

A 38 mm, 3 V LED filament from the ten-pack in the drawer, Kapton-taped flat under the module's front edge, glowing down. GPIO0 drives it through 68 Ω: about 12 mA, a soft 2200 K pool that spills out of the slot under the apron. Nothing is bought for the light. The same pin dims it, so it is also the only status light: barely there at rest, brighter while the button is held, a slow breath while the desktop thinks. One resistor, no transistor.

The desktop server that already exists

pocket_ai_assistant/character/server listens on ws://<desktop>:8790/ws, takes 16 kHz PCM, runs faster-whisper small.en and Ollama gpt-oss:20b (reasoning effort low) and sends the reply back as a transcript message. The hologram is one more client. Start the server with --host 0.0.0.0 so the Wi-Fi side can reach it; it stays on the home LAN, never port-forwarded.

How it sits with the family

Brass, glass, black and warm light. The Pocket AIs' and Cyber Dawn's brass, cut from the same K&S drawer, as a low plinth; one solid block of glass on it; black only in the display and the button; the 2200 K pool underneath. No paint, no skin, no ivory. At 75 mm wide it stands beside the 82 mm Cyber Dawn and the 70 mm Sphere at the same scale. One rectangle and four points: no rod beyond the outline. Prototype, not product.

What the firmware port needs

Not written yet; listed so the pin map above is known to be enough.

  • Start from pocket_ai_assistant/character/firmware (ESP-IDF 6.0.2, the C3 build): keep Wi-Fi, the WebSocket client, the 16 kHz I2S capture and the button logic; drop the amplifier (already a Kconfig switch).
  • Replace the I2C SSD1306 panel with the same esp_lcd SSD1306 driver on SPI (SSD1309 takes the same commands): pins 6/7/5/20/21, 4 MHz, 128 × 64, segment remap flipped so the reflection reads right.
  • Move the bit clock define from GPIO2 to GPIO3; console on USB Serial/JTAG, not UART0.
  • Render transcript messages as pages of text inside the middle 116 columns; show listening and thinking as a one-word state; discard the server's audio frames.
  • LEDC PWM on GPIO0 for the filament: pin low at boot, about 5 % at rest, 30 % while listening, a slow breath while thinking. The glow is the only status light.
  • Ask the server's character for two-sentence answers; the glass holds about 40 words a page.
  • Ported 2026-11-01 in desk_hologram/firmware/ (builds clean, never flashed). Rev C changes no pin and no line of it.

Before power, with the meter

  • Display header, before the controller is powered: pins 1 and 2 (GND and 3V) not shorted. A chirp that stops is the 220 µF charging.
  • Each of the five display signal wires beeps to its controller pin and to nothing next to it.
  • The brass floats, module glued in and Kapton on: frame to GND open, frame to 3V open. A beep is a header stub under the back bar or a pad at a leg; lift the module, look, re-tape.
  • Everything else is a supplier spec and is not metered.
  • At first power, not a meter check: read the Wi-Fi signal the firmware prints at boot with the frame off, then on. More than 10 dB lost means the 31 mm antenna wire from the Pocket AI's inventory note, pointed down at the slot, before any glue.

After approval

Order the two sticks of brass. Step 2, the physics page, exists for Rev A; its optics hold but for the 2.6 mm air gap, and its legs chapter (buckling, tipping) is redone for four straight brass legs.
